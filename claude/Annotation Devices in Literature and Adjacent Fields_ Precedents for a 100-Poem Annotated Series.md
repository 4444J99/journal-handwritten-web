# Annotation Devices in Literature and Adjacent Fields: A Research Report for a 100-Poem Annotated Series

The strongest precedents for this project are poets who added notes or reflections years after the poems: Coleridge's 1817 gloss, Wordsworth's 1843 Fenwick Notes, Eliot's Waste Land notes and his 1956 disavowal of them, Hejinian's age-indexed *My Life*, Tom Phillips's fifty-year *A Humument* and Bernadette Mayer's *Memory*. Across these cases, the late authorial note is never neutral. Readers end up treating it either as an authority that shuts the poem down or as a second character whose reliability is in question. A project that dates every layer and lets the layers disagree belongs in the second, more productive tradition, and can learn from the first tradition's failure modes.

## TL;DR

- **Late self-annotation always becomes a voice of its own.** Coleridge's 1817 gloss was later attacked as a "parasitic growth" (Empson). Eliot called his own notes "bogus scholarship" that "can never be unstuck." Wordsworth's Fenwick Notes, dictated in 1843 about poems written decades earlier, get dates wrong. Over time, readers come to read each of these as a dated persona, not a neutral key.
- **The most usable models for dated, accruing, self-contradicting layers** are Hejinian's *My Life* (1980, then 1987, then *My Life in the Nineties*), Phillips's *A Humument* (six editions, 1970s–2016), Dorst and Abrams's *S.* (ink colours as time-stamps), Brian Blanchfield's *Proxies* (2016, essays written from memory, then a "Correction" section) and Mayer's *Memory* (a 1971 journal later rewritten against photographs). Digital precedents include the Whitman Archive's edition-by-edition display, Storyspace hypertexts and Genius-style verified author annotation.
- **Design recommendation.** Keep the three note types visually and temporally distinct. Stamp every note with the date it was written as well as the poem's date. Never let a note announce what a poem "means." Treat contradiction between memory and the physical record as a feature to stage, not an error to resolve. The main risks are explanation that shuts the poem down, notes that take over from the poem, and a defensive tone, all documented below.

## 1. Map of the Traditions

Notes can be grouped by **who writes them** and **when**. Gérard Genette's *Paratexts: Thresholds of Interpretation* (*Seuils*, 1987; English translation 1997) gives the basic grid. On the enunciatory axis, a note is authorial, allographic (by someone else) or actorial (by a character). On the temporal axis, it is original, later (added for a second edition) or delayed (added much later). Genette calls the paratext "a zone between text and off-text, a zone not only of transition but also of transaction." He treats authorial notes as a separate class and devotes distinct sections to "original," "later" and "delayed" notes. He observes that later and delayed authorial notes, which are rarer, tend to do defensive commentary or self-criticism.\[1\]

| Relation to the note | Original (same time as the poem) | Later or delayed (years after) |
|---|---|---|
| **Author on own work** | Dante, *Vita Nuova* (prose and *divisioni* around his own poems); Marianne Moore's notes to *Observations* (1924); Eliot's notes (1922) | Coleridge's gloss (1817, about a 1798 poem); Wordsworth's Fenwick Notes (1843); Whitman's "A Backward Glance O'er Travel'd Roads" (1888); Henry James's New York Edition prefaces (1907–09); Auden's 1966 foreword; Moore's "A Note on the Notes" (1967); Eliot's "Frontiers of Criticism" (1956) |
| **Fictional or ironic voice** | E.K.'s glosses to Spenser's *Shepheardes Calender* (1579); Pope's *Dunciad Variorum* (1729); Swift's *Tale of a Tub* (notes added in 1710); Nabokov's *Pale Fire* (1962); Wallace's *Infinite Jest* (1996); Danielewski's *House of Leaves* (2000); Spicer's *After Lorca* (1957) | Borges's "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" (1940), with a postscript dated "1947"; *S.* (2013), with ink layers marking successive readings |
| **Another scholar or institution** | Scholia, the glossed Bible, the Talmud page; museum wall labels | Variorum editions (Shakespeare, Dickinson); Rainey's *Annotated Waste Land* (2006); the Waste Land app (2011); Whitman Archive; Genius |
| **Later self revising rather than annotating** | — | Wordsworth's *Prelude* (1799 / 1805 / 1850); Whitman's six *Leaves*; Yeats's revisions; Auden's revisions and deletions; Lowell's *Notebook* → *History* / *For Lizzie and Harriet* / *The Dolphin*; Hejinian's *My Life*; Phillips's *A Humument* |

The project combines three cells that are usually kept apart: author/original (the new 2026 poems with near-contemporary reflections), author/delayed (the 2014–15 journal poems with reflections written about ten years on), and author-as-scholar (the critical apparatus). Few precedents combine all three in one work. The nearest are Dante's *Vita Nuova*, Eliot's notes read together with his later disavowal, and Blanchfield's *Proxies*.

## 2. Key Case Studies

### A. Authorial self-annotation and retrospective commentary

**Dante, *Vita Nuova* (c. 1292–95) and *Convivio* (c. 1304–07).** This is the founding model of a poet writing prose around his own earlier lyrics. In the *Vita Nuova*, Dante places poems written over roughly a decade inside a retrospective prose narrative. Most poems are followed by *divisioni*, cool scholastic analyses of the poem's parts. These pull against the narrative's emotion, so the self-commentary is already split into memoir and analysis. The *Convivio* was planned as fourteen canzoni with prose commentary in fifteen treatises. Only four treatises were finished. It is a warning that heavy self-commentary can stall a project. Separately, Dante's authorship of the *Epistle to Cangrande*, a self-commentary on the *Paradiso*, is still disputed.

**Coleridge, the marginal gloss to *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* (1817).** The poem appeared anonymously in *Lyrical Ballads* (1798). It was first published under Coleridge's name in *Sibylline Leaves* (1817). That edition added a prose gloss printed in the margins, counted at fifty-eight glosses, and the gloss was slightly altered in 1828.\[2\] The *Literary Gazette*'s 1817 review mocked the poem as "whimsically indexed on the margin." Critics have read the gloss in three ways:
- **As explanation.** M. H. Abrams thought it was meant to "assist the bewildered readers."\[3\]
- **As distortion.** Empson called it a "parasitic growth"\[4\] and blamed "very misleading 'glosses'" on a middle-aged Coleridge's religiosity.
- **As a fictional layer.** Jerome McGann reads the glossed poem as "an evidently *mediated* text," an allegory of interpretation itself.\[3\] The British Library's introduction asks the key question: "is this Coleridge himself in the margin, or a fictional editor?"\[5\]

For this project, the gloss is the clearest case of a later self writing in a different register beside an earlier poem. Two centuries on, editors still argue over whether to print it.

**Wordsworth, the Fenwick Notes (1843) and the three *Preludes*.** From January to June 1843, aged 73, Wordsworth dictated notes on the circumstances of nearly all his poems to his friend Isabella Fenwick.\[6\] His daughter Dora and her husband Edward Quillinan copied them into a fair-copy notebook, finished 25 August 1843. Fenwick's original transcription does not survive.\[7\] The notes are both indispensable and unreliable. William Knight, the Victorian editor who printed them in full, warned that "his memory could not be absolutely trusted as to dates; and in some instances we know it to have been at fault."\[8\] He also said the true chronology could only be rebuilt by comparing the notes against Wordsworth's own lists, the editions and Dorothy Wordsworth's journal.\[9\] That is exactly the memory-versus-record friction this project wants to show.

Stephen Gill argued the notes "are best read entire, not as a source of information merely but as a document in Wordsworth's autobiography."\[10\] In other words, the late reflection has become a work in its own right. The standard edition is Jared Curtis, ed., *The Fenwick Notes of William Wordsworth* (1993; revised 2008).\[11\] The *Prelude*'s states — the two-part version of 1799, the thirteen-book version of 1805 and the fourteen-book version published posthumously in 1850 — show the other route: revising the poem instead of annotating it. Modern editions often print 1805 and 1850 on facing pages.

**Whitman, *Leaves of Grass* (1855–1891–92) and "A Backward Glance O'er Travel'd Roads" (1888).** Whitman kept one title for nearly forty years while rewriting, retitling, cutting and regrouping its poems. Ed Folsom writes that the book "ultimately went through six entirely different editions, and each edition had multiple issues."\[12\] Late in life Whitman added a retrospective prose essay, "A Backward Glance." It first appeared in *November Boughs* (1888) and was placed at the end of the "deathbed" edition of 1891–92.\[13\] This is a delayed authorial paratext that closes the book on the old poet's view of the young one.

**Yeats.** Yeats revised early poems heavily for his collected editions and added explanatory notes to several volumes. He defended the revising in a 1908 epigram: "The friends that have it I do wrong / Whenever I remake a song, / Should know what issue is at stake: / It is myself that I remake." The multi-volume Longman Annotated English Poets edition of Yeats now in progress exists partly because his own rearrangements "repeatedly revised and rearranged both texts" over time.\[14\]

**Marianne Moore, notes and "A Note on the Notes."** Moore attached notes naming the sources of quoted phrases from *Observations* (1924) onward. In the headnote to her *Complete Poems* (1967), "A Note on the Notes," she defended them: "since in anything I have written, there have been lines in which the chief interest is borrowed, and I have not yet been able to outgrow this hybrid method of composition, acknowledgements seem only honest."\[15\] She also gave readers permission to "disregard the notes."\[16\] Her notes record reading, not feeling: what she was copying from magazines, guides and treatises.\[17\] That makes them a direct precedent for documenting "what he was reading and copying that week." She also revised in her own printed copies all her life.\[18\] Robin Schulze's *Becoming Marianne Moore* (2002) gathers the variant versions published from 1907 to 1924.\[19\]

**Eliot, *The Waste Land* notes (1922) and their disavowal (1956).** The poem appeared without notes in *The Criterion* and *The Dial* in late 1922.\[20\] The notes were added for the Boni & Liveright book edition.\[21\] In "The Frontiers of Criticism" (1956) Eliot said he had first meant only to record his sources, "with a view to spiking the guns of critics of my earlier poems who had accused me of plagiarism." Then, because the poem "was inconveniently short," he expanded the notes "to provide a few more pages of printed matter, with the result that they became the remarkable exposition of bogus scholarship that is still on view today. I have sometimes thought of getting rid of these notes; but now they can never be unstuck."\[20\]

Treat the "padding" story with caution. It is Eliot's own retrospective account, given 34 years later in a lecture attacking source-hunting criticism. A. Walton Litz argued that the core notes "were never, of course, a hoax or a publisher's gimmick" and existed in private form while the poem circulated in typescript. On this reading the notes are "bogus" only "in their lack of proportion."\[22\] The tseliot.com compilation also records a contradictory remark, "The notes to the Waste Land should be taken at their face value."\[20\] The page does not give its date, so it is reported here unverified. The lesson is that notes cannot be withdrawn once published ("can never be unstuck"), and that the author's later view of his own notes becomes one more layer.

**Auden: revision and disowning.** For the 1945 *Collected Poetry* Auden cut a stanza of "September 1, 1939." He had first changed "We must love one another or die" to "and die."\[23\]\[24\] He then left the poem out of *Collected Shorter Poems 1927–1957* (1966).\[25\] In that book's foreword he explained he had discarded poems "because they were dishonest, or bad-mannered, or boring," defining "a dishonest poem" as one expressing "feelings or beliefs which its author never felt or entertained."\[26\] When he allowed it into a 1964 Penguin anthology, it carried a note that Auden "considers these five poems to be trash which he is ashamed to have written."\[25\] In a letter he called it "the most dishonest poem I have ever written."\[26\] Readers kept the original anyway; it circulated widely after 9/11.\[23\]\[27\] The later self's verdict did not override the early text. It became a famous paratext attached to it.

**Robert Lowell: *Notebook* → *History* / *The Dolphin*.** Lowell published *Notebook 1967–68* (1969) and a revised *Notebook* (1970). He then split and revised those sonnets into *History* and *For Lizzie and Harriet* and published both alongside *The Dolphin* in 1973.\[28\]\[29\] *The Dolphin* adapted, and sometimes changed, lines from Elizabeth Hardwick's private letters. Elizabeth Bishop's letter of 21 March 1972 objected that he was "violating a trust" and mixing fact with fiction: "*art just isn't worth that much*."\[30\]\[31\]\[32\] Saskia Hamilton's editions, *The Dolphin: Two Versions, 1972–1973* and *The Dolphin Letters, 1970–1979* (both 2019), now let readers compare versions with the documents behind them.\[30\]\[33\] Lowell is the cautionary case for a journal-based project: the "physical record" can include other people's words, and revising it raises ethical questions as well as aesthetic ones.

**Henry James, New York Edition prefaces (1907–09).** In 24 volumes James revised his earlier fiction and wrote eighteen retrospective prefaces about when, where and how each work began. This is the model of the late author returning to the scene of writing. The prefaces became criticism in their own right; R. P. Blackmur collected them as *The Art of the Novel*. James's heavy revisions of early novels such as *Roderick Hudson* remain controversial among editors.

**Lyn Hejinian, *My Life* (1980; 1987; *My Life in the Nineties*, 2003).** The structure is tied to the author's age. Poets.org and Thomas Fink say the 1980 Burning Deck edition had 37 sections of 37 sentences, for Hejinian's age when writing it. The 1987 Sun & Moon edition added eight sections and eight sentences to each existing section, making 45 × 45.\[34\]\[35\] *My Life in the Nineties* is a separate ten-section work with sixty-sentence paragraphs; Hejinian turned sixty in 2001.\[35\] *Conflicting sources:* The Nation's 2024 obituary says the original version had 38 sections of 38 sentences.\[36\] The figure of 37 is better supported, including by the 1987 edition's own afterword ("in her 37th year").\[35\] The 2013 Wesleyan edition collects the 1987 and 2003 texts but leaves out a related addendum and does not show 1980/1987 variants, a gap a 2024 *Transatlantica* article on the editions points out.\[37\] Here the poem, not a note, grows with the author, and the edition history itself becomes a layered record.

**Tom Phillips, *A Humument* (1966–2016).** Phillips "treated" every page of W. H. Mallock's 1892 novel *A Human Document*.\[38\] Tetrad Press first issued the pages (dates are given variously as 1970 and 1971–76), followed by Thames & Hudson trade editions in 1980, 1987, 1998, 2004 and 2012. Each replaced at least fifty pages. The sixth, "final" edition of 2016 contained no page from the first version.\[39\] Phillips then went back to the work anyway: at his death in 2022 he had made 78 pages of a third version.\[40\] This is the clearest model of versioning as the work itself, and of the claim to be "final" being overtaken by later work.

### B. Fictional, ironic and multi-voiced apparatus

- **E.K., *The Shepheardes Calender* (1579).** A prefatory epistle, "arguments" and glosses by the unidentified "E.K." frame Spenser's eclogues. They sometimes misread or over-explain the poems. Whether E.K. is Edward Kirke, Gabriel Harvey, Spenser himself or a mix is still *disputed*. The uncertainty is the point: a commentator whose authority cannot be pinned down.
- **Swift, *A Tale of a Tub* (1704; fifth edition 1710).** The 1710 edition added an "Apology" and footnotes partly taken from the hostile *Observations* of the critic William Wotton. Swift turned a critic's annotations into part of the satire.
- **Pope, *The Dunciad Variorum* (1729).** A mock-scholarly apparatus of prolegomena, "testimonies of authors" and notes attributed to "Martinus Scriblerus" and others swamps the poem on many pages. The satire works through the note layer.
- **Nabokov, *Pale Fire* (1962).** John Shade's 999-line poem is followed by Charles Kinbote's foreword, commentary and index. The commentary takes the poem over for Kinbote's own story. This is the canonical case of a note displacing the poem, and it works because it is staged as pathology.
- **Wallace, *Infinite Jest* (1996).** The novel has 388 endnotes, some with footnotes of their own.\[41\] Wallace's editor Michael Pietsch made him cut a great deal; Wallace said the endnotes once ran to "almost 400 pages."\[42\]\[43\] In a 1994 letter to Pietsch, Wallace gave his reasons. Endnotes let him "make the primary-text an easier read while at once":\[44\]
  1. allowing "a discursive, authorial intrusive style w/o Finneganizing the story";\[44\]
  2. mimicking "the information-flood and data-triage I expect'd be an even bigger part of US life";\[44\]
  3. giving "a lot more technical/medical verisimilitude";\[44\]
  4. making "the reader go literally physically 'back and forth' in a way that perhaps cutely mimics some of the story's thematic concerns";\[44\]
  5. letting him feel he was satisfying the request for compression "without sacrificing enormous amounts of stuff."\[44\]

  On *Charlie Rose* (27 March 1997) he described the notes as a way of disrupting the text's linearity without making it unreadable, and said writing needs "some interplay between how difficult you make it for the reader and how seductive."\[45\] Elsewhere he called notes "addictive," "a kind of call and response," and a way "to draw back a dimension, or do a meta-comment."\[42\] The physical flip is part of the design. Readers on forums still advise "DO NOT SKIP THE ENDNOTES" and complain that e-readers lose the bodily back-and-forth.\[46\] A tap-to-reveal interface removes exactly the friction Wallace wanted.
- **Danielewski, *House of Leaves* (2000).** Three annotators — Zampanò, Johnny Truant and anonymous "Editors" — are set in different typefaces. Footnotes nest, contradict each other and take over the page. Truant's notes become a memoir of his breakdown, an extreme version of the personal note growing into its own story.
- **Borges.** "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" (published 1940) ends with a postscript dated 1947, a later layer dated in the future that changes the story. "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" (1939) is invented scholarship about an identical text read differently because of its date. Both are exact precedents for dated layers changing how the same words read.
- **Spicer, *After Lorca* (1957).** Loose "translations" and invented poems are framed by an introduction signed by the dead Lorca and by letters from Spicer to Lorca. The apparatus is a dialogue with an absent author, and an example of correspondence used as a note.
- **Dorst and Abrams, *S.* (2013).** A fake library copy of the novel *Ship of Theseus* by "V. M. Straka," with "translator's" footnotes by "F. X. Caldeira," carries handwritten marginalia by two readers, Jen and Eric.\[47\] The marginalia are layered by ink colour that marks time:
  - Eric's early notes, in pencil, from before he meets Jen;\[48\]
  - first exchanges in blue (Jen) and black (Eric);\[48\]
  - then orange (Jen) and green (Eric);\[48\]
  - then purple (Jen) and red (Eric);\[48\]
  - finally both in black.\[48\]

  Notes from different times sit side by side on the same page, so readers rebuild the order from the colours.\[49\] Fan guides publish reading orders, and some readers report they could not tell the colours apart.\[50\]\[51\] This is the closest analogue to dated notes accruing around the same poem, and a warning that time-coding needs to be readable.
- **Anne Carson, *Nox* (2010).** An accordion-folded facsimile notebook in a box, made after her brother's death. Lexical entries for each word of Catullus 101 sit beside collaged fragments, photographs and memoir. The gloss (a dictionary entry) and the grief (the facing page) share each spread. It shows a personal reflection placed as a parallel layer, not a footnote.
- **Maggie Nelson, *Bluets* (2009).** 240 numbered propositions. Numbering lets digressive, self-correcting prose run in sequence without a host text, a model for endnotes that wander.
- **Brian Blanchfield, *Proxies: Essays Near Knowing* (Nightboat, 2016).** Blanchfield wrote each essay "with the internet off," with "a total suppression of recourse to other authoritative sources."\[52\] The book ends with "Correction," about twenty pages listing what his memory got wrong; for example, the word *owl* "appears in five other poems by Wallace Stevens."\[53\] Blanchfield calls it "a kind of monorail ride through the amusement park, above it all, perversely objective."\[54\] Reception was split. Hyperallergic reports he describes it as the book's most "lyrical" section.\[55\] The White Review found that the corrections, "divorced from their referents," made "dry reading" because they were unnumbered and unlinked.\[53\] A Rumpus interviewer admitted skipping it entirely.\[54\] This is the most direct contemporary precedent for letting memory and record contradict each other in public, and its reception shows that the contradiction needs to be linked to the exact point it corrects.
- **Bernadette Mayer, *Memory* (July 1971; text 1975/76; full edition Siglio, 2020).** Mayer shot one roll of 35mm film every day for a month, producing over 1,100 photographs, and kept a journal. According to Claire Grandy's peer-reviewed essay in *Post45* (October 2026), after the film came back, "sometimes weeks later, Mayer revised her daily journal entries while looking over the photographs, following tangents sparked by details in the images that redirect or overshadow her journal's written record." She then recorded herself reading the revised text; the installation was hung as a 36-by-4-foot grid with about eight hours of audio. Grandy reads the photographs as a "counter-memory" that "overwrite[s]" the written record. Siglio's 2020 edition is the first to print text and images together in full. It also changes the work: the earlier edition ended with an August "Dreaming" section, and Siglio's stops at July 31.\[56\]\[57\]\[58\]

### C. Critical apparatus and contextualizing frames

- **Scholia and the Talmud page.** Ancient and medieval manuscripts put commentary (scholia, glosses) around a central text. The printed Talmud, standardized by Daniel Bomberg's Venice edition (1520–23), places Mishnah and Gemara in the centre, Rashi's commentary on the inner margin and the Tosafot on the outer. Centuries of commentary appear at once, with fixed positions telling the reader who is speaking. It is the classic visual grammar for layers of different dates around one text.
- **Variorum editions.** H. H. Furness's New Variorum Shakespeare (from 1871) collects centuries of commentary under each line. For Dickinson, Thomas H. Johnson's variorum (1955) and R. W. Franklin's (1998) record her variant words and manuscript states; the Emily Dickinson Archive (2013) puts the manuscripts online. In a variorum, versions and commentary become the reading experience. Virginia Jackson's *Dickinson's Misery* (2005) warns that editorial and "lyric reading" practices can turn manuscript accidents into intended meaning, a caution for anyone publishing every journal edit.
- **The Waste Land app (Touch Press / Faber, June 2011).** It offers:
  - synchronized text;\[59\]
  - Fiona Shaw's filmed performance;\[59\]
  - readings by Eliot (from two periods of his life), Ted Hughes, Alec Guinness, Viggo Mortensen, and Jeremy Irons with Eileen Atkins;\[59\]
  - interactive notes;\[59\]
  - "over 35 expert video perspectives" (including Seamus Heaney and Jeanette Winterson);\[59\]
  - the manuscript facsimile with Pound's edits.\[59\]

  It won Best Adult App at the 2011 FutureBook Awards, and the Guardian's Apps Blog reported on 8 August 2011 that it earned back its costs in six weeks on the App Store. Faber reissued it for iPhone in 2022.\[60\] A 2013 *Digital Modernisms* blog post on Rickard A. Parker's hypertext "Exploring The Waste Land" noted that annotation "can also restrict hermeneutical potential" by unifying the text "along certain lines of interpretation." For this project, the app is proof of demand for layers revealed on request. It is also a warning: its many expert voices can turn the poem into a museum.
- **Museum wall labels.** Beverly Serrell's *Exhibit Labels: An Interpretive Approach* (1996; 2nd ed. 2015) is the field's standard guide.\[61\] Per a UCSB Library summary of the second edition, its rules include "All types of labels should be as short as possible," with caption labels of roughly 20–150 words.\[62\] Serrell herself reframes the question "How many words should there be in a label?" as "How many words does this label need to have?"\[63\] A wall label is consulted, not read straight through; it is subordinate to the object and written in an institutional, usually anonymous voice.\[64\] Catalogue raisonné entries add fixed fields (date, medium, provenance, exhibition history, literature), a structured model for an apparatus entry. *Unverified:* specific word limits (e.g., "50–75 words") often attributed to Serrell could not be confirmed here.
- **Genius-style annotation.** Poetry Genius (2013; folded into Genius in 2014) let readers annotate any line of a text. Verified artists can annotate their own work, with those notes highlighted.\[65\] Junot Díaz annotated a passage of *The Brief Wondrous Life of Oscar Wao* in July 2013 ("I like to think the mongoose was a visitor from another planet").\[66\] The T. S. Eliot Society and Etgar Keret also took part.\[67\]\[68\] This is the closest existing interface to tap-a-line-to-reveal-the-author's-note. Its risk is that crowd and author notes flatten into one interpretive layer.

### D. Theory and history of the paratext and footnote

- **Genette, *Paratexts*** (above). Its key tools for this project are the original/later/delayed distinction and the note as "transaction." Philippe Lejeune's line, quoted by Genette, is the core warning: the paratext is "a fringe of the printed text which in reality controls one's whole reading of the text."\[69\]
- **Anthony Grafton, *The Footnote: A Curious History* (Harvard, 1997; German original 1995).** Grafton shows the footnote as the historian's proof of work, a second voice where the text's authority is backed up or undermined. Gibbon kept his sharpest remarks for his notes. Grafton recounts that Hume urged Gibbon's publisher to move the notes from the end of the volumes to the foot of the page. Reviewers note the book is more a history of historiography than of the footnote as a device.\[70\]\[71\]\[72\]
- **H. J. Jackson, *Marginalia: Readers Writing in Books* (Yale, 2001).** Based on more than 2,000 annotated books, it shows readers writing to authors, friends, lovers and "future generations." Coleridge, the central figure, brought the word *marginalia* into English.\[73\] This is the reader-side history that *S.* fictionalizes.\[74\]\[75\]\[76\]
- **Revision and late style.** Useful framing comes from the long tradition of textual-scholarship debate over "final authorial intention." Lowell, Auden, Wordsworth and James's later revisions are standard test cases. It also comes from late-style criticism (Adorno; Edward Said's *On Late Style*, 2006) and from Andrew Epstein's *Attention Equals Life* (2016), which places Mayer's *Memory*, Hejinian's *My Life* and others in a 1970s "everyday-life aesthetics" of dated, diaristic writing.\[58\]
- **How readers actually experience notes.** The empirical evidence is thin and mostly not about literary reading. Ebsworth, McDonell, DeFazio and Cai ("Hypertext versus Footnotes," *IALLT Journal of Language Learning Technologies*, 2017) studied forty adolescent English-language learners and found "no significant difference between groups in recall" between hyperlinked and footnoted versions of a text. They contrasted this with Ebsworth and McDonell's earlier study of college students (*Journal of Multilingual Education Research*, 2013), in which both bilingual and native-speaker groups "recalled significantly more propositions with linear text than with hypertext." Eye-tracking work by Fitzsimmons, Jayes, Weal and Drieghe ("The impact of skim reading and navigation when reading hyperlinks on the web," *PLOS ONE*, 2020) found comprehension fell from about 91% on static pages to about 66% when readers could navigate freely by links in a "navigable Web environment." Chevet et al. (*Learning and Instruction*, 2021) found "more rereading behaviors when an interruption occurred" during digital reading but "no impairment in comprehension performance." The tentative conclusion is that optional, tap-revealed notes cost little comprehension in themselves. Free navigation away from the text costs more, and the bodily "back and forth" Wallace wanted is a design choice, not a neutral effect.

### E. Digital and hypertext precedents

- **Michael Joyce, *afternoon, a story*** (1987; Eastgate edition 1990, Storyspace). The work has about 539 lexias and about 951 links.\[77\] Links are unmarked: the reader looks for "words that yield to the touch."\[78\] "Guard fields" lock lexias until others have been visited (Alice Bell, 2010; Joyce, 1997).\[79\] Joyce described how such rules "can compound."\[80\] The precedent is reveal-on-demand without visible markers, and paths that depend on reading history.
- **Shelley Jackson, *Patchwork Girl*** (Eastgate, 1995). About 323 lexias and 462 links (Koskimaa).\[81\] Its five sections are story, graveyard, journal, crazy quilt and body of text.\[82\] The "crazy quilt" section links phrases to their sources (Baum, Shelley, Derrida, the Storyspace manual), a built-in critical apparatus that shows where borrowed text came from.\[83\]
- **The Walt Whitman Archive** (University of Nebraska–Lincoln; eds. Folsom, Price, Cohen). It presents each American edition of *Leaves* separately, from 1855 to the 1891–92 "deathbed" printing, with page images and named source copies.\[84\] It includes Whitman's own annotated 1860 "Blue Book," "with hundreds of Whitman's manuscript annotations, revisions, additions, and deletions."\[85\] It also hosts a colour-coded *1855 Variorum* (2019) covering about 200 surviving copies; Kenneth Price notes "It is possible that no two copies are identical."\[86\]\[87\] This is the best model for showing versions side by side with their sources visible.
- **Wikipedia-style page history.** Every revision is time-stamped, attributed and comparable, so the record of change is public and permanent. It is the infrastructure equivalent of what the poet wants: notes that accrue and stay dated.

## 4. Implications and Design Options for the Project

**What the precedents show.**
1. *Late notes become characters.* Coleridge's glossator, Wordsworth's 73-year-old dictating voice, Eliot's 1956 self-mockery and Auden's "trash" note all became personae that readers judge. The project should plan for this, not resist it. A 2026 reflection on a 2014 poem will be read as the voice of "the 2026 self," with its own blind spots.
2. *Notes cannot be withdrawn.* Eliot's "can never be unstuck" applies doubly to a digital work. If notes accrue, keep earlier notes visible (struck through or versioned, Wikipedia-style) and do not overwrite them, or the project loses the time-depth it is built on.
3. *Authority shuts meaning down.* Every apparatus that claimed to explain — Coleridge's gloss in the "explanatory" reading, the Waste Land notes as used by source-hunters, the app's experts — drew the complaint that it narrowed the poem. Apparatus that admits partiality (Moore's "disregard the notes," Blanchfield's "Correction," Kinbote's obvious bias) aged better.

**Concrete design options.**
- **Three distinct registers, each with a fixed position and typeface.** This follows the Talmud page and *House of Leaves*.
  - *Footnotes*: short, factual, anchored to a word, in the voice of a gloss.
  - *Endnotes*: numbered continuously across all 100 poems as Wallace's are, with room to digress and to point to other endnotes.
  - *Apparatus*: impersonal and structured like a wall label or catalogue entry, with fields such as date written, journal page, versions, sources and reading that week. Following Serrell, keep it as short as each entry needs.
- **Double dating on every note**, along the lines of *S.*'s ink colours and Borges's dated postscript. Each note carries two dates: the date of the poem or version it concerns, and the date the note was written ("on poem dated 2014-11-03 / written 2026-05-12"). This makes the different temporal distances visible: about ten years for the journal poems, near zero for the new ones. *S.* shows the risk: time codes must be legible (labelled dates, not colour alone).
- **Versions as a first-class layer.** Following the Whitman Archive and *A Humument*, show the journal page facsimile, each edit state and the published poem as a browsable sequence. Mayer's *Memory* suggests putting the document (page photo, the book he was copying) next to the reflection, so the record can quietly contradict the memory without the note having to say so.
- **Staged contradiction.** Blanchfield's model is to write the reflection from memory first, then check it against the dated pages and add a dated "correction" note. Unlike Blanchfield, link each correction to the exact sentence it corrects, to avoid the "divorced from their referents" problem the White Review identified. Wordsworth's editors had to rebuild his chronology from outside sources; the project can do this openly.
- **Accrual rules.** Decide in advance whether later reflections may comment on earlier reflections (endnote-within-endnote, as in *Infinite Jest*) and whether any note can ever be edited, as opposed to superseded. Hejinian's structure tied to age suggests a lighter option: a fixed date or interval for revisiting, which gives the accrual a shape.
- **Protect the poem on screen.** Tap-to-reveal removes Wallace's physical "back and forth." That is good for keeping the poem alone, but it lowers the sense of a second voice. Consider a small, consistent marker (Joyce's unmarked "words that yield" are too hidden for most readers) and a clear way back to the bare poem. The limited evidence suggests optional notes do not themselves hurt comprehension, but free navigation away from the text does.

**Failure modes to guard against.**
- *Over-explaining.* This is the reading of the Waste Land notes that Eliot regretted, and of the Coleridge gloss that Empson attacked. Rule: the apparatus says what a poem *references and when*, never what it *means*.
- *The note displacing the poem.* This is the *Pale Fire* and *Dunciad* pattern, effective only as deliberate satire. Rule: limit the length of footnotes and apparatus entries. Let only the endnotes wander, and keep them a tap further away.
- *Defensive or confessional tone.* Genette notes that late authorial notes tend to be defensive or self-critical.\[1\] Auden's "trash" and Eliot's "bogus scholarship" read as pre-emptive self-protection. Reflections that report circumstance (where, what reading, what was crossed out) age better than ones that judge the poem.
- *Ethical exposure.* Journal material may contain other people. Lowell's use of Hardwick's letters, and Bishop's "art just isn't worth that much," are the standard warning.\[88\]
- *False finality.* Phillips's "final" edition was followed by new pages, and Wordsworth's *Prelude* ended up as three works. Avoid calling any layer final.

## Caveats

- Several mechanics for B–C works (E.K., Swift's 1710 notes, Talmud layout, Borges's dating, *House of Leaves* typefaces, Dickinson variorums) come from standard literary-historical knowledge and were not freshly re-verified for this report. Check them against the editions before quoting.
- Eliot's "padding" story is his own retrospective account, and Litz disputes it in part. The remark "taken at their face value" is reported without a confirmed date.
- Hejinian's first-edition count (37 vs. 38) conflicts across sources; 37 is better attested.
- Dates for the first *Humument* printing differ by source (1970, 1971–76, 1973).
- The research on how readers experience footnotes and hyperlinks is small, mostly educational or web-based, and does not test literary or poetic reading. Treat its implications as suggestive.
- Contemporary (2000–2026) examples of poets publishing *reflective annotated editions of their own earlier poems* are rarer than expected. The closest found are Blanchfield, Hejinian's 2013 combined edition, Phillips's final *Humument* and Mayer's 2020 *Memory*, the last of which involved collaboration with her publisher, not new commentary. This gap is also an opportunity for the project.
