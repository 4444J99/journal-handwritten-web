# The Cut-Up and Its Family: Assembling New Writing From Writing That Already Exists

The cut-up is not a single trick that Burroughs invented. It is the most famous name for a practice at least 1,700 years old: making new texts by selecting, breaking apart and rearranging existing ones. Its modern forms run from Tzara's Dada hat (1920) through Gysin and Burroughs's scissors (1959–60s), Bowie's paper and software (1973–2016) and Yorke's top hat (1997–2003), into erasure, conceptual writing and sampling. The thing every serious practitioner admits, starting with Burroughs, is that the chance operation produces raw material and the author's *selection* produces the work. For a poet assembling poems from his own journal and its versions, that admission is the most useful thing in the tradition.

## TL;DR

- **Lineage:** the method goes back through Tzara's "To Make a Dadaist Poem" (1920), Cubist and Dada collage, and modernist montage (Eliot, Dos Passos, Joyce) to the Roman cento of the 2nd–4th centuries. Brion Gysin's accidental slicing of newspapers at the Beat Hotel in Paris (1959) made it a named, teachable method, and Burroughs turned it into a theory of language as control.
- **The three named figures:** Burroughs cut and folded texts to break "either/or" thinking and expose media control, but he openly said "I first made selections." Bowie used the cut-up on paper from 1973–74 (Diamond Dogs) and, from 1995 (Outside), through the Verbasizer, a Mac program built with Ty Roberts. Yorke pulled fragments of two years of writer's-block notes from a top hat for Kid A and Amnesiac (2000–01) and returned to it on Hail to the Thief (2003).
- **What it means for a self-sourced project:** the critical consensus is that cut-ups make meaning through curation, constraint and juxtaposition, not through randomness itself. A journal-and-versions project is closest to the "self-cut-up" practised by Cobain, Yorke and Burroughs (who recycled his own manuscripts). Recycling your own text raises no third-party rights issues, but quoting other writers does, and courts treat it very differently in music than in literature.

## Key Findings

1. **The origin story has a documented core and some uncertain edges.** The standard account is that Gysin noticed, while cutting mounts with a Stanley knife over layers of newspaper at the Beat Hotel (9 rue Gît-le-Cœur, Paris), that the sliced strips read across one another.\[1\] Accounts date this to autumn 1959, and Raphaël Haudidier's 2022 overview for the OELN network dates it to "late October." Burroughs's own 1965 *Paris Review* interview says he was "in Paris in the summer of 1960" when he took it up,\[2\] and Burroughs's essay says "the summer of 1959."\[3\] Treat the exact month as unsettled.
2. **Burroughs himself undercut the "pure chance" myth.** In the 1965 interview he said, "Somebody has to *do* the cutting up. Remember that I first made selections. Out of hundreds of possible sentences that I might have used, I chose one."\[2\] Oliver Harris's 2014 restored editions of the Cut-Up Trilogy, edited from the manuscripts, are presented by their publisher as demolishing "the myths of his chance-based writing methods."\[4\]
3. **Bowie is the best-documented songwriter in the tradition,** and he treated the method as a generator, not an oracle. He called it a "very Western tarot," and on camera in 1974 said he had actually used it on only a couple of songs.\[5\] He later built the method into software.\[6\]
4. **Yorke's method was explicitly therapeutic and practical.** It was a way of writing through writer's block, filling a top hat with "all the stuff that was in the writer's block that I didn't actually throw away."\[7\] Michael Stipe's coping advice is a separate thread: it became the seed of "How to Disappear Completely," a song Yorke wrote in 1997, not a cut-up.\[8\]
5. **The deepest precedent is the cento,** where Ausonius (4th century CE) set rules for line-length and joining, and Faltonia Betitia Proba, one of the few Latin women poets whose work survives, built a 694-line Christian poem entirely from Virgil. Constraint, not chance, was the organizing principle.
6. **Rights:** there is no copyright problem in cutting up your own unpublished work. With others' texts, literature has historically been tolerant in practice but not in law (the Kathy Acker–Harold Robbins affair, 1989). Music became strict after *Grand Upright* (1991) and *Bridgeport* (2005).

## Details

### 1. The core figures

#### William S. Burroughs (1914–1997) and Brion Gysin (1916–1986)

**The discovery and the method.** Gysin moved into the Beat Hotel in 1958 after closing his Tangier restaurant, The 1001 Nights. Burroughs returned there from London in September 1959, and Gysin showed him the technique.\[9\] Gysin's own rationale, repeated in Burroughs's essay "The Cut-Up Method of Brion Gysin" (1961, revised for *The Third Mind*), was that "writing was fifty years behind painting."\[1\] The essay says the cut-up "brings to writers the collage, which has been used by painters for fifty years."\[10\] It describes the basic method as taking a page, cutting it into four sections, and rearranging them ("Now cut down the middle and cross the middle"). It also claims that "all writing is in fact cut-ups."\[3\]

**First publications.** *Minutes to Go* (1960; Gysin, Burroughs, Sinclair Beiles, Gregory Corso) was presented as "unedited unchanged cut ups."\[10\] The Cut-Up or Nova Trilogy followed: *The Soft Machine* (1961, heavily revised 1966 and 1968), *The Ticket That Exploded* (1962, revised 1967) and *Nova Express* (1964).\[11\] In the 1966 *Soft Machine* revision Burroughs removed 82 pages, inserted 82 new ones and re-cut the remaining 100.\[12\] The book was literally re-versioned by its own method. *The Ticket That Exploded* relies especially on the **fold-in**.\[13\] *The Third Mind*, the joint "book of methods," appeared in French in 1976 and in English in 1978\[14\] (it is often cited as 1977, so the date varies by source).

**The fold-in.** Burroughs described it simply: "instead of cutting, you fold."\[2\] A page of one text is folded vertically and laid against a page of another, so the reader reads across both. His story "They Just Fade Away" is "a fold-in from *Lord Jim*." He told the *Paris Review* that *Nova Express* contains Joyce, Shakespeare, Rimbaud, Kerouac, Kafka, Eliot and Conrad, and that "when you start making these fold-ins… you lose track."\[2\]

**Why he did it.** His stated reasons, in his own words:
- **Breaking linear logic.** He called the "either/or proposition" "one of the great errors of Western thought," and said "cut-ups are a movement toward breaking this down."\[2\]
- **Perception.** "Cut-ups make explicit a psychosensory process that is going on all the time anyway": we read the adjacent newspaper columns subliminally.\[2\]
- **Expanding vision.** "Cut-ups establish new connections between images, and one's range of vision consequently expands."\[2\]
- **Getting past repression.** He argued you cannot reach the same result by free association: "The mental mechanisms of repression and selection are also operating against you."\[2\]
- **Control and divination.** In the wider Burroughs/Gysin corpus the cut-up becomes a weapon against the "word virus" and media control, and a form of prophecy: "When you cut into the present the future leaks out."\[15\] Treat this as Burroughs's mythology, not a demonstrable claim.

**Scrapbooks, three-column notebooks, "time travel."** In the 1965 interview Burroughs describes:
- pasting newspaper clippings and photographs beside passages of his own manuscripts in scrapbooks;\[2\]
- keeping travel notebooks in three columns: what happened, what he remembered, and quotations from whatever book he was reading;\[2\]
- calling the whole practice "time travel."\[2\]

He also says he had "literally thousands of pages of notes."\[2\] Scholarship on "the Word Hoard," approximately 1,000 typewriter pages written mainly in Tangier in 1954–58 (as established in Oliver Harris's introduction to *The Soft Machine: The Restored Text*), shows he recycled the same typescripts across *Naked Lunch*, *Interzone* and *The Soft Machine*. In other words, **Burroughs's most important source material was his own earlier writing**, which is the closest historical analogue to a journal-based project.

**Tape and film.** With the engineer Ian Sommerville, Burroughs and Gysin extended the method to tape-recorder cut-ups. Some of these date to 1959 and were released by Industrial Records as *Nothing Here Now but the Recordings* (1981).\[16\]\[17\] They also made the film *The Cut-Ups*, with Antony Balch, which opened in London in 1967.\[15\] Balch had his footage cut into pieces and reassembled without control.\[15\] Gysin and Sommerville's Dreamachine (early 1960s), a flicker device meant to be "viewed with eyes closed," belongs to the same project of bypassing habitual perception.\[18\]

**Lineage claims.** Burroughs named his precursors himself: "*The Waste Land* was the first great cut-up collage, and Tristan Tzara had done a bit along the same lines. Dos Passos used the same idea in 'The Camera Eye' sequences in *U.S.A.*"\[2\] His essay adds a legend that is **not reliably documented**: that Tzara proposed pulling a poem out of a hat "at a surrealist rally in the 1920s," that "a riot ensued," and that Breton expelled him.\[19\] Tzara and Breton did fall out bitterly in the early 1920s, but this specific scene is Burroughs's dramatization.

#### David Bowie (1947–2016)

**Encounter.** On 17 November 1973, *Rolling Stone* brought Bowie and Burroughs together for a joint conversation. Bowie had immersed himself in *Nova Express* beforehand.\[20\] The magazine published the piece in early 1974.

**Paper method, 1974 onward.** Bowie used cut-ups on *Diamond Dogs* (recorded early 1974), on the title track and the "Sweet Thing/Candidate/Sweet Thing" medley according to secondary accounts.\[20\] In Alan Yentob's BBC documentary *Cracked Actor* (filmed 1974, broadcast 1975) he demonstrates cutting up a page on camera. He says, roughly, that he doesn't know if this is how Burroughs or Gysin do it but this is how he does his, calls it "a western form of Tarot"\[1\]\[20\] and says it ignites "anything that might be in my imagination."\[6\] He also admits he had used it on only a couple of songs.\[5\] In a 2008 interview (quoted secondhand) he described writing "a paragraph or two describing different subjects," a kind of "story ingredients" list, then cutting the sentences "into four or five-word sections" and reconnecting them.\[21\] Biographer David Buckley says Bowie would also write a song in both first and third person and randomize the two perspectives to create "a 'new' subjectivity."\[6\] This is a self-cut-up of point of view, not just words.

**The Verbasizer (1995).** Ty Roberts had made interactive CD-ROMs for Bowie and Brian Eno through his company ION (1992–94). Visiting the studio during the *Outside* sessions, he saw Bowie cutting up "multiple word sources, from the newspaper to hand-written words," throwing them into a hat, arranging fragments on paper, and then "cross[ing] out material that didn't fit." He offered to write software to speed this up.\[6\]\[22\] In Michael Apted's documentary *Inspirations* (1997), Bowie demonstrates the program on a PowerBook:\[6\]
- he divides sentences across columns, "sometimes I'll go as much as 20, 25 different sentences";\[6\]
- he sets it to randomize, so it "cut[s] in between them all the time, picking out… different words from different columns, and from different rows";\[6\]
- the result is "a real kaleidoscope of meanings and topic and nouns and verbs all sort of slamming into each other."\[6\]

Roberts said Bowie sometimes went from the computer to the microphone with new material within minutes. Roberts later co-founded Gracenote (1998), and his team rebuilt a news-fed "Verbasizer 2.0" for the *David Bowie Is* exhibition.\[22\] Some popular sources say the Verbasizer shaped Bowie's writing all the way to *Blackstar* (2016).\[23\] That claim is **not well documented** and should be treated as speculation.

**Eno and Oblique Strategies.** Brian Eno and the painter Peter Schmidt published *Oblique Strategies: Over One Hundred Worthwhile Dilemmas* in 1975. It is a deck of aphoristic prompt cards drawn at random to break creative impasses, combining Schmidt's *The Thoughts Behind the Thoughts* (1970) with Eno's own cards (1974).\[24\]\[25\] Eno used them during Bowie's "Berlin" albums *Low* and *"Heroes"* (1977) and *Lodger* (1979), where sessions included swapping instruments and playing old songs backwards.\[26\]\[27\] The distinction matters. The cut-up randomizes **material** (the words). Oblique Strategies randomizes **procedure** (what you do next). Bowie's 1976–79 work combined both, and *Outside* (1995) reunited Bowie and Eno with the software version.\[6\] One commentator notes that Yoko Ono's instruction pieces of the 1960s anticipated the card idea.\[25\]

#### Thom Yorke / Radiohead

**Context.** After the *OK Computer* tour (1997–98), Yorke had severe writer's block and something close to a breakdown. During the *Kid A*/*Amnesiac* sessions (1999–2000) his songs were sometimes little more than a drum-machine rhythm and lyric fragments drawn from a hat.\[28\]

**Method, in his words.** In a radio interview with Steve Lamacq, Yorke explained that he had "a writer's block for two years, and all the stuff that was in the writer's block that I didn't actually throw away instantly was put into this top hat." Whenever the band was jamming and he "didn't know what to sing, I'd pull stuff out of the hat." He added that "you used to get some great stuff." On the title track he said the keyboard was "singing and all I'm doing is mouthing words that I'm picking out of a hat."\[7\] He used vocal processing to distance himself from material he found too violent to sing straight.\[28\] "Idioteque" is also documented as cut-up-derived.\[29\] *Kid A* (2 October 2000) was Radiohead's first album since 1993 without printed lyrics, because Yorke felt the words could not be separated from the music.\[28\] For *Hail to the Thief* (2003) he "returned to the method of cutting up words and arranging them randomly" for some songs, partly because there was no time to rewrite in the studio.\[30\]

**The key point for a journal project.** Yorke's hat was filled with **his own discarded writing**. It was a self-cut-up of a notebook.

**Michael Stipe.** This is a separate, well-documented strand. Stipe gave Yorke a coping phrase for tour stress, and Yorke built the 1997 song "How to Disappear Completely" (released on *Kid A*) around it. He mentioned the chorus in a July 1997 interview, and Stipe later called to apologize for "stealing the concept" when he used it himself.\[8\] This is an example of **quotation and echo** (taking a friend's spoken line), not chance. It shows how close cut-up sits to the older practice of borrowing.

### 2. Lineage and precursors

**Tristan Tzara (1896–1963).** "To Make a Dadaist Poem" appears in the "Dada Manifesto on Feeble Love and Bitter Love," dated 12 December 1920.\[31\] The recipe: take a newspaper and scissors, cut out an article, then cut out each word, shake them in a bag and copy them "conscientiously in the order in which they left the bag."\[32\] The punchline is ironic: "The poem will resemble you," making you "an infinitely original author of charming sensibility."\[33\] Tzara is mocking the cult of originality as much as proposing a method. Hans Arp's collages "arranged according to the laws of chance" (c. 1916–17) are the visual twin.\[32\]

**Surrealism.** The Surrealists turned Dada chance inward, toward automatism (Breton's *Manifesto*, 1924). They also made it collective in the *cadavre exquis* or exquisite corpse (c. 1925), a parlour game in which players add words or drawings to a folded sheet without seeing the previous contributions. Burroughs's jibe that Breton "grounded the cut-ups on the Freudian couch" points at this split between chance as an external operation and chance as unconscious expression.\[3\]

**Visual collage and montage.**
- Picasso and Braque introduced *papier collé* and collage into Cubism in 1912.
- Kurt Schwitters built *Merz* works (from 1919) and sound poetry out of refuse and printed scraps.
- Raoul Hausmann described the moment Berlin Dada saw that "one could… make pictures, assembled entirely from cut-up photographs" (photomontage, c. 1918–19).\[34\] Hannah Höch's *Cut with the Kitchen Knife Dada through the Last Weimar Beer-Belly Cultural Epoch of Germany* (1919–20) is the landmark woman-authored example.
- In film, Eisenstein's theory of montage (1920s) holds that meaning arises from the collision of shots. Godard's jump cuts and his late *Histoire(s) du cinéma* (1988–98) make cinema out of quotations.

Gysin's "fifty years behind painting" is literally the gap between 1912 collage and 1959 cut-ups.

**Modernist assemblage in literature.**
- Mallarmé's *Un coup de dés jamais n'abolira le hasard* (1897) scattered type across the page and put "chance" in its title.
- Eliot's *The Waste Land* (1922) is built from quotations in several languages, ending in "These fragments I have shored against my ruins," with notes that act as a source list.
- Pound's *Cantos* (1915–1962) collage letters, histories and translations.
- Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922) and *Finnegans Wake* (1939) absorb and remix found language.
- Dos Passos's *U.S.A.* trilogy (1930–36) alternates fiction with "Newsreel" sections (montages of headlines and song lyrics) and the "Camera Eye" stream-of-consciousness passages that Burroughs specifically named.
- Walter Benjamin's unfinished *Arcades Project* (1927–40) is a vast, ordered assemblage of quotations. Benjamin dreamed of a work made almost entirely of citations.

**The cento.** A cento (Latin for "patchwork cloak") is a poem made entirely of lines or half-lines from an earlier poet.
- **Hosidius Geta's** *Medea* (late 2nd–early 3rd century CE), a 462-line tragedy, is the earliest known Virgilian cento.
- **Proba's** 694-line Virgilian cento (4th century) retells Genesis and the Gospels.
- **Empress Eudocia** (5th century) is associated with the Homeric centos on Christian themes.
- **Ausonius's** *Cento Nuptialis* (4th century) comes with a prefatory letter that is the only surviving ancient statement of the rules. Fragments may be cut at any caesura, no borrowed section should exceed a line and a half, and two consecutive lines should not be taken. He compares the work to the *stomachion*, a puzzle of fitting pieces, and admits breaking his own rules three times.\[35\]

The cento proves that **assembly under constraint** is ancient, and that its pleasure lies in recognizing sources. It also lies in hearing new meaning wrung from old words, often "against the grain" of the original.

**Commonplace books and bricolage.** From antiquity through the Renaissance and into the 19th century, readers copied striking passages into commonplace books organized by topic, to be recombined in their own writing. This was the pre-modern equivalent of Burroughs's scrapbooks. Quilting gives the cento its name and remains a living metaphor; Lethem calls consciousness itself "stitched, quilted, pastiched." Claude Lévi-Strauss's *The Savage Mind* (1962) contrasted the *bricoleur*, who builds from whatever is at hand, with the engineer, who procures materials for a plan. The *bricoleur* works with "a heterogeneous repertoire" of leftovers whose earlier uses still cling to them. That is a precise description of composing from a journal.

**Chance and rule-based writing.**
- **Jackson Mac Low** began using chance operations with "5 biblical poems" (December 1954 to January 1955). He developed "acrostic read-through text-selection" (mostly 1960–63) and "diastic reading-through text-selection" (from 1963), in which a seed word's letters are used to pick words from a source text in sequence. *Stanzas for Iris Lezak* uses acrostics and diastics. His stated aim was "to lessen… the hegemony of the ego of the artist."\[36\]\[37\] He co-published, with La Monte Young as editor, *An Anthology of Chance Operations* (1963), a key Fluxus-era document.\[38\]
- **John Cage** wrote mesostics, which are like acrostics but spell a name down the *middle* of the lines. He used I Ching–derived chance operations to select from source texts.\[39\]\[40\] *Writing through Finnegans Wake* (1978, University of Tulsa / *James Joyce Quarterly* supplement) runs mesostics on "James Joyce" through the entire *Wake*, followed by second through fifth "writings through" (1978–83).\[41\]\[42\]\[43\] *Roaratorio* (1979) is the sound piece built from it.\[44\] Some sources say Cage's mesostic grew out of Mac Low's diastic; this is plausible but not confirmed here.\[45\]
- **Oulipo** (Ouvroir de littérature potentielle) was founded in 1960 (24 November, by most accounts) by Raymond Queneau and François Le Lionnais. It privileges *constraint* over chance. Jean Lescure's S+7 (N+7) "consists of replacing each noun… with the seventh noun found after it… in a dictionary."\[46\]\[47\] Raymond Roussel's procedural novels and his posthumous *How I Wrote Certain of My Books* (1935) are acknowledged ancestors, and Georges Perec's lipogrammatic *La Disparition* (1969) is the famous result.\[35\] J. A. Lindon's "vocabularyclept" poems rearrange *all* the words of an existing poem into a new one, often keeping meter and stanza.\[15\]

**Erasure, blackout and found poetry.**
- **Tom Phillips** read the 1965 Burroughs *Paris Review* interview, experimented with "column-edge" poems cut from the *New Statesman*, and set himself a rule: the first coherent book he found for threepence would serve. On 5 November 1966 that was W. H. Mallock's *A Human Document* (1892). He painted over most of each page to leave new texts, creating a character, Bill Toge, who exists only where "together" or "altogether" occurs.\[48\]\[49\]\[50\] *A Humument* appeared first in 1970, in trade form in 1980 (Thames & Hudson), and in a "final" edition in 2016 after every page had been reworked at least twice.\[49\]\[50\]\[51\] **It is the clearest model of a single source re-versioned over decades.**
- **Ronald Johnson's** *Radi os* (1977) erases Books I–IV of *Paradise Lost* down to a visionary poem. **Doris Cross's** *Col\*umns* (1982) worked on dictionary pages.\[52\]\[53\]
- **Jen Bervin's** *Nets* (2004) leaves Shakespeare's sonnets faintly visible beneath bolded survivors.\[54\]
- **Mary Ruefle's** *A Little White Shadow* (2006) erases a Victorian book.\[53\]
- **M. NourbeSe Philip's** *Zong!* (2008) cuts apart the legal decision about the 1781 massacre on the slave ship *Zong*.\[52\]
- **Austin Kleon's** *Newspaper Blackout* (2010) brought the form to a mass audience.\[53\]
- **Srikanth Reddy's** *Voyager* (2011) erases Kurt Waldheim's memoir three separate times (2003–09).\[54\]
- Travis Macdonald (*The O Mission Repo*, 2008, from the 9/11 Commission Report) and Janet Holmes (*The ms of my kin*, 2009, from Dickinson) extend the form. **Jordan Abel** and **Billy-Ray Belcourt** use erasure to mirror the erasure of Indigenous peoples.\[52\]\[55\]
- **Alicia Cook** publishes her own poems alongside blackout versions of them.\[55\] This is a direct precedent for self-erasure.

Reddy's remark that after *Voyager* he would "never do another erasure again" is a useful warning about how exhausting single-source methods can be.\[56\]

**Conceptual writing and appropriation.** Kenneth Goldsmith's *Day* (2003) retypes the entire *New York Times* of 1 September 2000 into 836 pages.\[57\] His *Uncreative Writing: Managing Language in the Digital Age* (Columbia, 2011) argues that context and choice are now the content.\[58\]\[59\] The 1970s–80s Language poets (*L=A=N=G=U=A=G=E* magazine, 1978–81; Ron Silliman's "New Sentence"; Charles Bernstein; Lyn Hejinian) foregrounded the materiality of language and the parataxis that cut-ups produce. Jonathan Lethem's "The Ecstasy of Influence: A Plagiarism" (*Harper's*, February 2007) is a modern cento: an essay on influence assembled largely from uncredited quotations, with a key to its sources at the end. Kathy Acker (1947–1997) called her rewriting of *Great Expectations*, *Don Quixote* and *Treasure Island* "piracy" and named Burroughs as her model.\[60\]

**Music.**
- **Musique concrète** (Pierre Schaeffer, from 1948) and tape-loop work are sonic cut-up before the name existed. Holger Czukay of Can spliced tape into rock in the late 1960s–70s.\[61\]
- **Industrial music:** Genesis P-Orridge corresponded with Burroughs from around 1970. P-Orridge said "there would have never been industrial music without cut ups," describing Throbbing Gristle (1975–81) cutting in "sounds from the street, factories, people screaming, television, radio." Burroughs told P-Orridge, "Your task is to find ways to short-circuit control."\[62\]\[63\] Later, P-Orridge and Lady Jaye's "pandrogyne" project extended the "third mind" idea to bodies and gender, which is a major queer chapter of the tradition.\[63\] Cabaret Voltaire (named after the 1916 Dada club in Zurich) built their early work from tape loops and cut-up sound.\[64\]
- **Hip-hop:** sampling and DJ cutting are widely discussed as a musical cut-up. The connection is mostly analogical rather than a documented influence; a hip-hop lyricist quoted in Beatdom's "William Burroughs – Heavy Metal Guru" remarks that "most people in the rap world" aren't "hip to the cut-ups," though if they "checked out Burroughs and Gysin they'd certainly see the connections."
- **Kurt Cobain** told Jim DeRogatis (*Chicago Sun-Times*, 1993, around the release of *In Utero*): "My lyrics are total cut-up… I take lines from different poems that I've written. I build on a theme if I can, but sometimes I can't even come up with an idea of what the song is about."\[65\]\[66\] He also wrote in his journals that he wrote songs "from pieces of poetry thrown together."\[67\] Cobain recorded "The 'Priest' They Called Him" with Burroughs (1993).\[68\] DeRogatis, not Cobain, made the explicit Burroughs connection.\[66\] **Cobain is the clearest songwriter precedent for building songs from one's own notebook poems.**
- **Others:** Iva Davies of Icehouse deliberately fragmented "Great Southern Land" (1982) with cut-ups, and the sleeve of the album *Primitive Man* shows torn strips of handwritten lyrics.\[15\] Claims that Mick Jagger used cut-ups on *Exile on Main St.* are reported only as "purportedly" in popular sources and are **unverified**.\[21\]

### 3. Practitioners you may not have thought of

- **Harold Norse** wrote *Beat Hotel* (written 1960s) with cut-ups, at the source.\[69\]
- **Claude Pelieu and Carl Weissner** spread the cut-up into French and German underground writing in the 1960s–70s. Weissner was Burroughs's German translator.
- **Alan Burns** (UK) wrote *Europe After the Rain* (1965) by folding pages and reading across columns, "discovering for myself many of the techniques Burroughs and Gysin describe."\[15\] This is independent convergence.
- **Herta Müller** (Nobel 2009) has published collage poems made from words and letters cut out of newspapers and magazines and printed in facsimile: *Die blassen Herren mit den Mokkatassen* (2005), *Vater telefoniert mit den Fliegen* (Hanser, 2012, containing 187 colour collages, 73 of them translated by Thomas Cooper as *Father's on the Phone with the Flies*, Seagull Books, 2018) and later volumes (2019, 2021, 2023). This is a major non-Anglophone woman practitioner. Austrian radio Ö1 compared her collage verses to children's counting-rhymes, and Harald Hartung in the *Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung* (2012) heard "allegories of power" in their rhyming play, rooted in Müller's experience of dictatorship and Securitate surveillance.
- **Eduardo Paolozzi's** *Kex* (1966), a "collage novel" cobbled from crime novels and newspaper clippings, is cited by Lethem.\[70\]
- **Kathy Acker**, **Genesis P-Orridge**, **M. NourbeSe Philip**, **Proba**, **Hannah Höch**, **Doris Cross**, **Mary Ruefle** and **Jen Bervin** give a strong female and queer counter-lineage to the usual all-male Burroughs–Bowie–Yorke line.
- **Austin Kleon** and **Alicia Cook** represent the popular, social-media-era blackout form. Generative and AI text work is the contemporary computational frontier, and Srikanth Reddy has written directly on "AI language models and erasure poetry."\[71\]

### 4. Theory and criticism

**Why it works (the main arguments).**
- **Intertextuality.** Julia Kristeva (coining the term in 1966–67) held that every text is "a mosaic of quotations." Roland Barthes's "The Death of the Author" (1967) says a text is "a tissue of quotations drawn from the innumerable centres of culture," with meaning made by the reader, not the author. On this view the cut-up only makes visible what all writing already is, which is exactly Burroughs's claim that "all writing is in fact cut-ups."\[3\]
- **Bricolage** (Lévi-Strauss, 1962) frames the method as legitimate making with what is at hand, where materials carry traces of their former uses.
- **Defamiliarization and juxtaposition.** Montage theory and Burroughs's "new connections between images" argue that collision produces a third meaning neither fragment holds alone. That is the "third mind" Gysin and Burroughs attributed to collaboration itself.\[16\]
- **Bypassing the censor.** Burroughs, Mac Low ("hegemony of the ego") and Eno's cards all hold that chance outflanks habit, cliché and self-censorship.

**Skeptical views.**
- **Meaning comes from selection.** Burroughs conceded it ("I chose one"), and Harris's manuscript scholarship shows heavy authorial shaping of the trilogy.\[2\]\[4\] Critics say the randomness is overstated and the result is ordinary authorship with a romantic alibi.
- **Hard to tell from editing.** Ty Roberts saw Bowie "cross out material that didn't fit." Yorke kept only what was "great stuff."\[6\]\[7\] Once a writer filters, rearranges and revises chance output, the end product can look like ordinary editing, and readers usually can't tell which lines came from the hat.
- **The writer as editor or curator.** Goldsmith embraces this ("managing language"). Critics counter that it can collapse into mere arrangement, or into privilege, since appropriation is easier to celebrate when the appropriator holds cultural power. The ethics of erasure are hotly argued around texts of atrocity, though *Zong!* is generally read as a model of handling them responsibly.
- **Monotony.** Even admirers note that pure cut-up prose can become uniform. Burroughs moved back toward more narrative work after the trilogy, and the *Nova Express* reception treated it as "the end of Burroughs's stylistic experiment."\[72\]
- **Mysticism.** The divinatory claims ("the future leaks out") are not testable. Cut-ups do not predict anything. What they do reliably is generate surprising raw material.

### 5. Practical method variations (described, not demonstrated)

- **Standard cut-up (Burroughs/Gysin):** take one or more pages, cut each into quarters, swap the quarters, and read straight across.\[3\] Alternatively, cut into strips of a few words, then pick up and lay them down.
- **Fold-in:** fold page A lengthwise and align it against page B, then read across the seam. This works best with two texts of different registers, such as a diary page and a news page.
- **Hat-draw (Tzara/Yorke):** cut to single words or short phrases, draw blind, and either copy in order (Tzara's strict rule) or keep only what sparks (Yorke's practical rule).\[7\]\[32\]
- **Column or cross-reading:** read across newspaper columns without cutting (Burroughs's *Nation* demonstration), or across the three columns of a notebook (event / memory / reading).\[2\]
- **Bowie's "story ingredients":** write short paragraphs on unrelated subjects, cut them into 4–5-word units and recombine.\[21\] A variant writes the same material in first and third person and shuffles the two.\[6\]
- **Programmatic (Verbasizer):** enter source sentences in rows, split them into columns, and randomize across rows within columns. Free web imitations exist.\[73\]
- **Statistical text generation:** n-gram and Markov-chain "travesty" generators learn which words follow which in a source and produce new text with the source's local texture. Hugh Kenner and Joseph O'Rourke's *Travesty* program (*BYTE*, 1984) is a classic example. Fed only one's own corpus, these produce plausible "you-sounding" sentences that have never been written.
- **Diastic and mesostic (Mac Low/Cage):** use a seed word to select words from a source mechanically. This gives a constraint with a traceable procedure.
- **Erasure or blackout:** keep the page and remove most words. Survivors keep their original order, which makes erasure more "faithful" to the source's sequence than the cut-up.
- **Cento:** assemble only from existing lines, under an explicit rule such as Ausonius's (no fragment longer than a line and a half, never two consecutive lines).
- **Self-cut-up and revision as cut-up:** Burroughs's Word Hoard, his 1966 *Soft Machine* re-cut, Yorke's top hat of discarded writer's-block notes, Cobain's lines "from different poems that I've written," Phillips's lifetime re-treating of one book, and Alicia Cook's self-blackouts. All of these treat **one's own earlier text as the source**.

**How practitioners choose among results.** Burroughs said "out of hundreds of possible sentences… I chose one," and that "you learn to leave out words and to make connections."\[2\] Bowie looked for collisions and crossed out what "didn't fit."\[6\] Yorke used what he needed when he "didn't know what to sing."\[7\] Phillips followed a self-imposed rule but chose which words survived. The shared criterion is *energy and surprise that still fits a governing theme, sound or emotional need*. Cobain: "I build on a theme if I can."\[66\]

### 6. Rights and attribution (information, not legal advice)

- **Your own material:** cutting up your own unpublished journal raises no third-party copyright issue. You are the rights-holder of every version.
- **Literature:** cut-up and appropriative literature has mostly lived in a tolerant gray zone. The landmark dispute is **Acker v. Robbins (1989)**. In July 1989 *Publishers Weekly* revealed that around four pages of Harold Robbins's *The Pirate* (1974), a sex scene, appeared in Acker's *The Adult Life of Toulouse Lautrec* (1975), reprinted in *Young Lust* (1989). Robbins's UK publisher Hodder & Stoughton asked Acker's publisher Unwin Hyman to "withdraw and pulp *Young Lust*," and Acker was pressed to apologize. Accounts differ on the outcome: some say Acker was forced into a public apology, while others say that after Burroughs intervened, Robbins granted retroactive permission.\[60\]\[74\]\[75\] Both may be true in sequence. Acker said she had "lost her belief that there can be art in this culture."\[76\]
- **Music:** *Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros.* (S.D.N.Y., 17 December 1991; Biz Markie's sample of Gilbert O'Sullivan) opens "Thou shalt not steal" and is credited with ending "the days of casual sampling."\[77\]\[78\]\[79\]\[80\]\[81\] *Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films* (6th Cir., 2005) went further: "Get a license or do not sample," even for tiny fragments of sound recordings.\[61\]\[82\] This makes the songwriting branch of the tradition legally tighter than the literary one.
- **Fair use generally (US):** it turns on purpose and transformation, the nature of the work, the amount used and market effect. Erasure of public-domain texts (Milton, Shakespeare, Mallock, Victorian novels) avoids the issue entirely, which is one practical reason erasurists favour old sources. Cutting up government documents (the 9/11 Report, legal decisions) also generally carries low risk in the US.
- **Attribution norms:** centos traditionally signal their source (Virgil). Eliot added notes. Bervin and Phillips name their sources in the title or apparatus. *Zong!* cites the legal case. Lethem appended a key to his borrowings. Credit is the community norm even where the law is silent.

## Closing: questions and options for a journal-and-versions project

These are framings, not decisions:

- **Which ancestor fits each phase?** Assembling the 100 poems from journal fragments sits in the **self-cut-up** line (Burroughs's Word Hoard, Yorke's hat, Cobain's own poems). Keeping every edit as a version sits in the **Phillips** line, one source reworked across decades. The later "roots and glyphs" phase sits closest to Gysin's calligraphic *écritures*, Burroughs's interest in hieroglyphs ("a whole block of associations"), concrete poetry and Oulipian letter-constraints.
- **Chance or constraint?** Would a given poem be better served by a random operation (hat, fold-in, Verbasizer-style shuffle), by a rule (cento-style limits, diastic seed words, erasure of a single journal page), or by a prompt about *procedure* (Oblique Strategies)?
- **What is the "second text"?** Fold-ins work through register collision. Options include the 2014–15 journal folded against the 2020s revision of the same page, against a found text, or against a quoted writer's line. The last makes it a cento-like echo and raises attribution choices.
- **How will selection be made visible?** Since critics locate the art in selection, would publishing the version history alongside each weekly poem (as Bervin leaves Shakespeare visible, or as Alicia Cook pairs poem and blackout) turn the editing itself into part of the work?
- **How will borrowed lines be credited?** For lines echoed from other writers, the options run along a spectrum: Eliot-style notes, Lethem-style end key, cento-style "after X," or keeping borrowings in the public domain.
- **What's the governing theme?** Following Cobain's "I build on a theme if I can," would each poem or batch need a theme or emotional need to choose against, so that the pile of possibilities doesn't become the "monotony" critics warn about?
- **When does the method stop?** Reddy's vow never to erase again, and Burroughs's return to narrative after the trilogy, suggest building in exits. Some weeks could be cut-up, others conventional revision, with the version archive recording which was which.
