# Writing the World's Scripts Through 100 Poems: A Learning Roadmap, Rendering Modes, Technical Notes and Rights Cautions for Anthony

Learn scripts first and languages second, and treat each script as a visible layer on a poem rather than as a translation. You can learn to read and handwrite most alphabets, abjads and abugidas in a few weeks each. True poetic translation into a second language takes years: the U.S. Foreign Service Institute estimates about 1,100 class hours for Russian, Hindi or Hebrew and 2,200 for Japanese, Arabic or Korean just to reach professional working proficiency.\[1\] So for the first year your honest, self-made modes are transliteration, homophonic play, calligraphic rendering and invented-script work. Full translation should wait for one deep language studied over time, plus credited native-speaker collaborators. Translations and transliterations also count for your publication strategy. Many journals treat any online appearance as prior publication, including of a translation, so any poem in the Reserve should stay offline in every script.\[2\]

## TL;DR

- **Script ≠ language; scripts take weeks, languages take years.** Use the writing-system typology (alphabet, abjad, abugida, syllabary, morphographic, featural) as the organizing lens for the series. Learn about a dozen scripts in order of increasing structural distance from Latin (Greek → Cyrillic → Hebrew → Arabic → Hangul → Devanagari → kana → others), and run kanji as a slow parallel track. Commit to only one or two languages deeply enough to translate.
- **Your honest modes depend on your stage.** Weeks 1–52: transliteration, homophonic translation (after the Zukofskys' *Catullus*), handwritten calligraphic renderings and Xu Bing–style invented scripts, all your own creative work. Year 2 onward: self-translation into your deep language. Throughout: collaborative translations by credited native speakers, shown as clearly labeled guest layers. AI may drill, check and look things up, but must never supply the text.
- **The rights rules split.** The U.S. Copyright Office does not treat online display alone as "publication," but journals such as *Poetry* say translations "that have appeared online for any reason… are considered to have been previously published." A GRUW may include derivative works such as translations. Keep Reserve poems offline in every script, and register originals before posting any version. This is informational only, not legal advice.

## Key Findings

### 1. Script vs. language: the distinctions that matter

**Facts:**
- A *language* is what you speak or write, and a *script* is the visual system that encodes it. Sanskrit is a language. Today it is usually printed in Devanagari, which also writes Hindi, Marathi and Nepali, but historically it was also written in other Brahmic scripts.\[3\] Kanji (漢字, "Han characters") are Chinese characters borrowed into Japanese, and they are one of three Japanese scripts, alongside hiragana and katakana. Cyrillic is a script family that serves Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Kazakh and others. Hebrew script writes Hebrew but also historically Yiddish and Judeo-Arabic. A review of Daniels & Bright notes that "Hebrew script used for Judeo-Arabic still functioned as an abjad; Hebrew script used for Yiddish or Sephardic Spanish functions as an alphabet."\[4\] Script type therefore depends partly on how a script is used, not only on what it looks like.
- Peter T. Daniels' typology, introduced in 1990 and developed in *The World's Writing Systems* (Daniels & Bright, Oxford, 1996), is the standard modern framework.\[5\]\[6\] In it:
  - A **logosyllabary/morphographic** system writes morphemes plus syllables (Chinese hanzi, Japanese kanji).
  - A **syllabary** writes syllables or morae (Japanese kana, the Cherokee syllabary).
  - An **abjad** writes consonants, with vowels optional (Hebrew, Arabic).
  - An **alphabet** writes consonants and vowels equally (Greek, Latin, Cyrillic, Georgian, Armenian).
  - An **abugida** has base consonants with an inherent vowel, modified by marks (Devanagari, Ge'ez/Ethiopic).
  - A **featural** system has shapes that encode phonetic features (Korean Hangul).
  Daniels coined "abjad" from the Arabic letter order and "abugida" from the Ge'ez letter order, the latter on Wolf Leslau's suggestion.\[5\] Scholars dispute the edges of the scheme: Bright preferred "alphasyllabary," and the featural category, which contains essentially only Hangul, is contested.\[4\]\[6\]
- Unicode 17.0 (released September 9, 2025) encodes 159,801 characters across 172 scripts.\[7\]\[8\] Nearly any script you choose is digitally representable.

**Interpretation (why the taxonomy suits your project):** Every typological category is really a different theory of what a "unit" of language is: sound segment, syllable, consonantal root, morpheme or articulatory feature. Your series already breaks poems down (versions, footnotes, the roots-and-glyphs phase), so the typology gives you a principled *classification* axis. Poem n/100 can be "carried" into a script type, and the academic note can explain what that type foregrounds. An abjad hides your vowels, an abugida fuses consonant and vowel, a syllabary chops your lines into morae, and kanji force a choice of *meaning* over sound. That is precisely the "classify and qualify and expand" you described.

### 2. Recommended script set and order

**Recommendation.** Order the scripts by structural distance from Latin, so each one teaches a new principle:

| # | Script | Type | Approx. inventory | Rationale | Est. time to read + handwrite slowly* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Greek | Alphabet | 24 letters | Ancestor of Latin and Cyrillic; ties to rhetoric and English roots | 1–2 weeks |
| 2 | Cyrillic (Russian set) | Alphabet | 33 letters | Shows one script adapted to many languages; "learnable in a few days"\[9\] | 1–2 weeks |
| 3 | Hebrew (square + cursive) | Abjad, RTL | 22 letters + 5 final forms, optional niqqud | First RTL script; vowel-optional writing | 2–3 weeks |
| 4 | Arabic | Abjad, RTL, cursive-joining | 28 letters, up to 4 positional forms | Shaping/joining; calligraphic depth | 3–5 weeks\[10\] |
| 5 | Hangul | Featural alphabet in syllable blocks | 24 basic jamo | Designed script; featural principle; block composition | 1 week\[11\]\[12\] |
| 6 | Devanagari | Abugida | ~47 base letters + matras + conjuncts | Inherent vowel, conjuncts; Sanskrit/Hindi | 3–5 weeks |
| 7 | Hiragana + katakana | Syllabaries (moraic) | 46 + 46 basic | Prerequisite for any Japanese; vertical text | 1–2 weeks |
| 8 | Georgian (Mkhedruli) | Alphabet | 33 letters | Unrelated letterforms, unicameral, beautiful round ductus | 1–2 weeks |
| 9 | Armenian | Alphabet | 38 letters (classical 36 + 2) | Single-inventor tradition (Mesrop Mashtots) | 1–2 weeks |
| 10 | Ge'ez/Ethiopic | Abugida (fidel) | ~26 bases × 7 orders | Origin of the word "abugida"; vowel-order grids | 3–4 weeks |
| 11 | Cherokee syllabary | Syllabary | 85 characters | Invented by Sequoyah (completed c. 1821); Latin-looking shapes with unrelated values | 2–3 weeks |
| 12 | Tifinagh (Neo-Tifinagh) | Alphabet (historically abjad) | ~33 letters | Geometric Amazigh script; revival politics | 1–2 weeks |
| ∞ | Kanji | Morphographic | 2,136 Jōyō kanji | Run as a parallel 6–12-month track | months\[13\] |

*These are estimates for adult self-study at about 30–45 minutes a day, to the point of slow decoding and legible copying, not fluent reading. They are extrapolated from the sources below and are not measured figures. Treat them as planning numbers, not facts.

Why this order: Greek and Cyrillic give fast wins and set up the roots phase. Hebrew and then Arabic introduce right-to-left direction and the abjad principle, with Arabic adding cursive shaping. Hangul is the easiest "alien" script. Devanagari and Ge'ez teach the abugida. Kana prepares the long kanji track. Georgian, Armenian, Cherokee and Tifinagh broaden the project beyond the usual "exotic" set, and each has a strong origin story that suits an academic note.

### 3. Learning: what the evidence supports

**Facts:**
- **Handwrite to learn.** Adults who learned novel characters by handwriting rather than typing were better at recognizing them, especially their orientation (Longcamp, Boucard, Gilhodes & Velay, 2006).\[14\]\[15\] fMRI work (Longcamp et al., 2008) found that handwritten learning engaged different brain areas than typing did.\[15\] The evidence is not unanimous: a 2022 *Journal of Cognitive Psychology* study assigned primary school children to "a handwriting, a touch typewriting or an alphabet tuition control group" and found no advantage over alphabet tuition alone. For adults learning *novel* shapes, the evidence still favors the pen, which also suits your handwriting-centered project.
- **Mnemonics for kanji.** Heisig's *Remembering the Kanji* gives each kanji an English keyword and builds a story from its components. It teaches meaning and writing first and readings later.\[16\]\[17\] Heisig claims a full-time learner could finish in "four to six weeks."\[18\] A paper in ERIC ED454822 (ICCE/ICCAI 2000 conference papers, Taipei) states, "In only 200 hours, Heisig claims, adults can learn the kanji." Learners' reports are mixed. Some find it fast and durable, while critics say it delays real Japanese, and some learners start confusing kanji after a few hundred.\[17\]\[19\] The Jōyō list has 2,136 characters since the 2010 revision, which added 196.\[13\]\[20\] His companion *Remembering the Kana* claims about 3 hours each for hiragana and katakana.\[21\]
- **Hangul really is fast.** Jeong Inji's postface to the 1446 *Hunminjeongeum Haerye* claims that "a wise man can acquaint himself with them before the morning is over; even a stupid man can learn them in the space of ten days."\[22\]\[23\] That is hyperbole, but it matches modern learners' experience of reading Hangul within days.\[24\]
- **Language competence takes far longer.** FSI estimates class hours to professional working proficiency (S-3/R-3). Category I (Spanish, French) needs 600–750 hours. Category III (Russian, Hindi, Hebrew, Greek, Turkish) needs "approximately 44 weeks (1100 class hours)," per the State Department's FSI page. Category IV (Arabic, Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese, Korean) needs about 2,200 hours or 88 weeks of *full-time* intensive instruction. The figures for Greek and Hebrew vary across sources, which place them in Category III.\[25\]

**Interpretation:** Script literacy and translation competence differ by roughly two orders of magnitude. At about 45 minutes a day, roughly 275 hours a year, a Category III language is a four-year project and a Category IV language an eight-year project before professional-level reading. This is not a reason to skip translation. It is a reason to (a) pick **one** deep language now, (b) be explicit in each poem's note about which mode you used, and (c) bring in collaborators for real translation in other languages.

**Recommendation on the deep language:** Ancient or Modern Greek is the best fit for a rhetorician. It feeds the etymology of English rhetorical and scholarly vocabulary, it is FSI Category III, and Perseus-type resources support close reading. Choose Japanese only if kanji is your central passion; it is Category IV and a much longer road.

**Resources (recommendation):**
- Anki, with handwriting-first cards (see the glyph, write it on paper, then check).
- Heisig *RTK* plus *Remembering the Kana*.
- KanjiVG stroke-order data for drills.
- Calligraphy primers for Arabic (naskh before thuluth) and Hebrew (square and cursive).
- For Devanagari, a Sanskrit or Hindi primer that teaches conjuncts explicitly.
- Daniels & Bright (1996) and Coulmas as reference spines.
- Get stroke-order sheets from native-education sources rather than random apps.

### 4. Modes of carrying a poem into another script

| Mode | What it is | Precedent | Doable by you, honestly, when? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transliteration** | English sounds written in another script (e.g., your line in Cyrillic or Hangul) | Common in language teaching; Hebrew script for Yiddish shows scripts crossing languages | **Week 1 onward.** Fully your own; label it "transliteration, not translation" |
| **Homophonic translation** | New English, or another language, following the *sound* of a source | Celia & Louis Zukofsky, *Catullus* (worked on 1958–66, published 1969): "Miss her, Catullus?" for *Miser Catulle*\[26\]\[27\] | **From the start**, inverted: you can homophonically "English" a foreign text, or play your own lines against another language's sounds |
| **Calligraphic/visual rendering** | The handwritten form *as* the poem | Arabic, Chinese and Hebrew calligraphy traditions | **After about 4–8 weeks per script.** Be humble: present these as study pieces, not mastery |
| **Invented-script / pseudo-script** | Shapes that look like a script but encode something else, or nothing | Xu Bing, *Book from the Sky* (1987–91; per the Blanton Museum of Art, Xu "invented 4,000 pseudo-characters and carved them into wooden printing blocks") and *Square Word Calligraphy* (1994–, English words built into Chinese-style square characters, with classroom installations where viewers learn to write it) | **Anytime.** Highly suited to you: a "Square Word"-style hybrid of your own Latin hand is original and avoids passing off fake text as a living script |
| **Concrete/constrained** | Script used as visual material; constraints such as "abjad version: delete vowels" | Concrete poetry (Gomringer, de Campos brothers) | **Anytime.** The typology itself generates constraints (abjad = vowel-less English, syllabary = moraic lineation) |
| **Translingual/multilingual poetry** | Poems mixing languages and scripts | Theresa Hak Kyung Cha, *Dictee*; Cecilia Vicuña | **Year 1+**, using words you actually know |
| **Self-translation** | The poet translates their own work | Beckett (French↔English), Nabokov | **Year 2–4+** in your deep language; label drafts as learner translations |
| **Collaborative translation** | Native speaker/translator translates, you co-edit | Standard literary-translation practice | **Anytime**, credited as their creative work (see §5) |

**Interpretation:** The Zukofsky precedent matters most to you. Josef Horáček argues that the *Catullus* "never abandons semantic correspondence but rather redefines it."\[28\] Charles Bernstein says that "the mark of the translator be pronounced."\[27\] Both legitimize a learner's translation that is strange, sound-driven and openly partial. You should make that partiality *explicit* in each poem's Study view rather than hiding it.

### 5. Collaboration and ethics

**Recommendations:**
- **Participation model.** Invite native speakers or translators to contribute a "guest layer" for a specific poem. Credit them by name, on the layer and in the endnote, as translator. Agree in writing (a short email license) on rights, payment or honorarium, and where it may appear. Journals like *Poetry* pay "the translator for the translation, and the original author… for the originals," which is a good norm to copy.\[29\] Translator credit also has a legal basis: a translation is a derivative work, and its copyright belongs to the translator unless assigned.\[30\]\[31\]
- **Your no-AI rule fits cleanly.** Translations are creative writing, so they must be yours or a credited human collaborator's. AI may legitimately help in these ways:
  - Generating and scheduling drills.
  - Checking your handwriting against stroke order.
  - Explaining grammar.
  - Acting as a dictionary or morphological parser.
  - Flagging errors in a translation you wrote. Record "checked with X" in the technical notes, and never paste in AI suggestions as text.
  - Writing code for shaping, layers and font pipelines.
  Keep a per-poem process log so the provenance is auditable.
- **Sacred scripts.** Hebrew, Arabic and Devanagari/Sanskrit carry liturgical weight:
  - Avoid writing divine names, such as the Tetragrammaton in Hebrew or Qur'anic phrases in Arabic, in playful or distorted forms.
  - Avoid pseudo-Arabic or pseudo-Hebrew "decorative" text. Unlike Xu Bing's frame, which invents a script the viewer is told is invented, fake versions of living sacred scripts read as exoticism.
  - Have a native reader check anything presented as real text before publication. Mis-shaped Hebrew and Devanagari are common and very visible errors.
  - Describe these scripts in your academic notes as living community scripts, not ornaments.
- **Avoid tokenism.** Each script layer should do structural work, which the typology lens provides, rather than serve as a decorative "world" flourish.

### 6. Technical notes

**Facts:**
- **Vertical text.** Use `writing-mode: vertical-rl` for Japanese, Chinese and Korean, and `vertical-lr` for Mongolian.\[32\]\[33\] Pair it with `text-orientation: mixed | upright`.\[34\] W3C notes that Arabic set upright in vertical text "will show unjoined letters," so do not stack RTL scripts vertically.\[32\] Set `lang` on every element, because browsers use it to choose line-breaking rules. Flexbox and grid axes rotate along with `writing-mode`.\[34\]
- **RTL text.** Use the HTML `dir="rtl"` attribute rather than CSS alone; W3C recommends that HTML authors use `dir`.\[35\] Isolate mixed-direction runs (`<bdi>`, `unicode-bidi: isolate`) so English footnote markers don't scramble Hebrew or Arabic lines.
- **Complex shaping.** Arabic letters take up to four contextual forms.\[10\] Devanagari needs half forms, conjunct ligatures and reordering (the short-i matra is written *before* its consonant).\[36\] Browsers handle this through OpenType shaping (HarfBuzz) *if* the font contains the features. Noto (Google's free family covering most scripts) gives matched fallbacks: Noto Sans/Serif Hebrew, Noto Naskh Arabic, Noto Sans Devanagari, Noto Sans/Serif JP, and others.
- **Glyph counts and feasibility of extending your handwriting font:**

| Script | Glyphs needed for a usable handwriting font | Shaping work | Feasibility via Calligraphr-style tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greek | about 50–70 (upper/lower + final sigma + tonos) | none (final ς is a separate code point) | Easy\[10\] |
| Cyrillic | about 66–80 | none | Easy (Calligraphr offers Cyrillic and Greek templates)\[37\]\[38\] |
| Hebrew | 27 + optional niqqud | mark positioning if you use niqqud | Easy–moderate |
| Hangul | 11,172 precomposed syllables, or 51 jamo + composition rules | heavy (block composition) | Hard by hand; possible with jamo + scripting |
| Arabic | 28 letters × up to 4 forms ≈ 100–150 + ligatures | init/medi/fina/isol + lam-alif | Moderate–hard; Calligraphr needs "manual glyph linking," with variable results\[37\] |
| Devanagari | commercial fonts ship about 750–900 glyphs (ITF's Volte/Quantum about 750; Akhand 892); Sanskrit-complete Chandas has 4,347 | half forms, conjuncts, reph, matra reordering | Hard; a FontForge plan budgets about 12 weeks to reach 900 glyphs with native-reader feedback\[39\]\[40\]\[41\]\[42\]\[43\] |
| Kana | about 180 incl. small/voiced forms | none | Moderate |
| Kanji | 2,136 (Jōyō) | none | Impractical by hand; render as calligraphy *images* instead\[13\] |

  Calligraphr's free plan allows "up to 75 characters" with 2 variants each; the Pro plan allows up to 600 characters (per MakeUseOf and WeFonts). A proper editor (Glyphs, FontForge) is needed for Arabic and Devanagari OpenType features.
- **Recommendation: model scripts as an orthogonal axis.** Treat script layers as an axis separate from edit versions.
  - Data model: `Poem → Version (v1…vn) → Rendering {mode, script, language, author, status, date, publicStatus}`.
  - A rendering always points to the specific version it was made from, because a Cyrillic transliteration of v3 is stale once v4 exists. Flag stale renderings rather than silently updating them.
  - In the interface, the version stack stays the z-axis, and scripts become a lateral carousel or a "script ring" around the poem, consistent with your hidden-menu rule (tap to reveal).
  - Include a `publicStatus` field (`reserve | registered | public`) so a Reserve poem cannot be exported in *any* script.
  - The Hand↔Font view extends naturally: your handwriting next to Noto, per script.

### 7. Publication and rights (informational, not legal advice)

**Facts:**
- **Legal "publication" ≠ journal "publication."**
  - The Copyright Office's Compendium (Third) §1008.3 states that "the Office does not consider a work to be published if it is merely displayed or performed online, unless the author or copyright owner clearly authorized the reproduction or distribution."\[44\] It also notes that courts are not bound by this view and have differed.\[45\]
  - Journals apply a stricter *editorial* standard. *Poetry*'s translation guidelines say "Translations that have appeared online for any reason, including social media posts, are considered to have been previously published."\[29\] *Poetry Northwest* excludes work published "on personal blogs and social media."\[46\] *The New Yorker* excludes work posted on "websites and personal blogs, even if a posting has been removed."\[47\]
  - Translation venues mostly disqualify a prior *translation*, not the original. Asymptote says "Translations must not have been previously published, although the source text may have been."\[48\] *The New Yorker* says "(The original-language text may have been published elsewhere.)"\[47\]
- **Self-translation policies vary.**
  - WWB's 2021 Poems in Translation Contest said "Self-translations are eligible."\[49\]
  - The Fitzcarraldo/New Directions/Giramondo Poetry in Translation Prize accepts "self-translated work."\[50\]
  - *Transference* says "we do not accept self-translations."\[51\]
  - *The Bombay Literary Magazine* treats self-translations "as original works in English."\[52\]
- **Copyright in self-translations.**
  - A translation is a derivative work, and copyright in a derivative work covers only the new material (17 U.S.C. §103(b); Circular 14).\[53\]\[54\]
  - Under 37 CFR §202.4(c)(3), a GRUW group "may include individual works, joint works, or derivative works," up to ten works by the same author, all unpublished and in the same administrative class.\[55\]\[56\]\[57\]
  - The Compendium (§1008.2) excludes previously published material from a derivative claim, even when the claimant authored it.\[44\]

**Interpretation and recommendations:**
1. **Reserve poems: no script versions online at all.** A Hangul transliteration or a calligraphic Arabic rendering of a Reserve poem could still be read as "appeared online" by strict editors. Keep these renderings in the offline build only.
2. **Register before posting.** Register originals by GRUW, ten per application. Once you post a poem, it may count as "published" if downloads are enabled, and published works are not eligible for GRUW.\[58\]\[59\]\[60\] Disable downloads or prints of poem pages if you want to keep display-only status, but don't rely on that with journals.
3. **Translations need their own registration only if they carry substantial new authorship and you want the statutory benefits.** A pure transliteration (mechanical sound mapping) may add little copyrightable authorship. A real translation or homophonic version is new authorship. If both are unpublished, you can put the original and its translation in *separate* slots of the same GRUW. Ask a copyright attorney before relying on any of this.
4. **Collaborators own their translations** unless they assign them, so get it in writing.

**Opportunities (verify current cycles):**
- **NEA Translation Project Fellowships:** $10,000–$25,000 for published translators translating *into* English.\[61\]\[62\] Outbound self-translation of your own poems won't qualify, but translating others' work into English later could. The FY2026 deadline was January 16, 2025, and aggregators list January 2027 for the next one. Only one Literature Fellowship application is allowed per year, and FY2027 Creative Writing fellowships are in poetry.\[61\]\[63\]\[64\]
- **PEN/Heim Translation Fund:** ten $4,000 grants for book-length translations into English. The 2026 deadline was June 15, 2026.\[65\] It fits your "translate others' work later" goal.
- **Electronic Literature Organization:** the Robert Coover Award ($1,000) suits the interactive web piece, and the N. Katherine Hayles Award ($1,000) suits criticism. 2026 nominations closed March 9, 2026, so expect a spring 2027 window.\[66\]\[67\]
- **NEH Fellowships** (up to $60,000; next deadline April 14, 2027) could fund the scholarly "roots and glyphs" apparatus if framed as humanities research.\[68\]
- **Prize for self-translated collections:** the Fitzcarraldo/New Directions/Giramondo Poetry in Translation Prize.\[50\]

### 8. Feeding the "roots and glyphs" phase and per-poem academic notes

**Recommendations:**
- **Academic note template per poem:**
  - (a) the script type used and what it foregrounds;
  - (b) the script's history (e.g., Phoenician abjad → Greek alphabet → Latin/Cyrillic; Brahmi → Devanagari; Sejong's 1443/1446 Hangul; Sequoyah's syllabary);\[22\]
  - (c) the etymology of two or three key words, tracing English → Latin/Greek → PIE roots;
  - (d) what was lost or gained in the rendering.
- **LingFrame integration:** add a grapheme layer alongside morphemes. Use Unicode character properties (script, joining type, general category), Unihan and IDS (ideographic description sequence) component data for kanji decomposition, and KanjiVG stroke SVGs (copyright Ulrich Apel, licensed openly).\[69\] Store grapheme→phoneme mappings per script so transliteration layers are *generated by your own rules*. That is technical writing, and therefore AI-permissible, while the poem text remains yours.
- **Scholarship spine:**
  - Daniels & Bright, *The World's Writing Systems* (1996);
  - Daniels, "Writing Systems" (Blackwell *Handbook of Linguistics*);
  - Sampson, *Writing Systems* (1985) on the featural category;
  - DeFrancis, *Visible Speech* (1989);
  - Share & Daniels (2016), *Writing Systems Research*, on aksharas and abugidas;\[70\]
  - Coulmas, *Writing Systems* (2003);
  - Horáček, "Pedantry and Play: The Zukofsky *Catullus*" (*Comparative Literature Studies* 51.1, 2014) for translation theory.\[28\]

## Recommended Roadmap (fits one poem per week)

**Daily (30–45 min):**
- 15 min Anki review of glyphs, handwriting-first.
- 15–20 min copying with stroke order on gridded paper.
- 10 min reading real text in the current script (signage, children's books, a news headline).

**Weekly cycle aligned with poem n/100:**
- **Mon–Tue:** edit poem n in English (version stack).
- **Wed:** make one script rendering of poem n in the *current* script, in the stage-appropriate mode (transliteration first, then homophonic or calligraphic).
- **Thu:** write the academic note section on that script and the poem's roots.
- **Fri:** native-reader check (a collaborator or an exchange partner) for anything presented as real text. Log any AI checks.
- **Weekend:** deep-language study block (2–3 hrs) and handwriting-font work.

**Year 1 schedule (about 4 poems per script block):**
- Weeks 1–4: Greek, plus pick a deep language and start it.
- Weeks 5–8: Cyrillic.
- Weeks 9–14: Hebrew.
- Weeks 15–22: Arabic.
- Weeks 23–25: Hangul.
- Weeks 26–33: Devanagari.
- Weeks 34–37: kana, with Heisig kanji starting at about 10–15 new characters a day.
- Weeks 38–52: Georgian, Armenian, Ge'ez, Cherokee and Tifinagh, plus a Xu Bing–style invented-script poem of your own design.

**Year 2+:** self-translations into your deep language, collaborative guest layers in two or three other languages, and handwriting-font expansion (Greek → Cyrillic → Hebrew first; Arabic and Devanagari only with a proper font editor).

## Caveats

- The per-script time estimates are informed extrapolations, not measured data. FSI figures are for full-time intensive classroom instruction aimed at professional proficiency, and sources disagree slightly on category boundaries (e.g., 1,012 vs. 1,100 hours; Greek's placement).\[25\]
- The research on handwriting vs. typing is strongest for novel shapes in adults and older children. Some classroom studies find no advantage.\[71\]
- The grant and prize cycles listed are from 2025–26 pages. Confirm current guidelines before planning.
- The publication and copyright points are informational, not legal advice. The Compendium is the Office's view, not binding on courts, and journals set their own rules. Check each venue's current guidelines.
- I could not verify every journal's self-translation policy. Most major translation venues (Asymptote, MPT, *Poetry*) say nothing explicit, so query editors directly.
