# Business Models for a Numbered Handwritten-Poetry Series: A Comparative Report

**Bottom line:** For this project, the best model is free public posting of the n/100 series to build an audience, combined with a reserve of never-posted poems kept for literary credibility. Money should come from three sources: a low-priced membership that sells the version and notes layers, small editions of physical objects for each numbered poem, and a crowdfunded collected edition after the series ends. The font, NFTs and paywalled access to the web piece should be secondary or skipped. One distinction drives the whole strategy: copyright "publication" and journal "previously published" are different tests. Posting on social media usually doesn't make a poem "published" for copyright purposes, but it does for most top journals.

## TL;DR
- **Lead with free posting, and earn money through layers and objects.** Membership access to versions, drafts and notes (Patreon or Substack, about 10% platform fee plus processing), small numbered editions of prints or handwritten fair copies for each poem ($25–$125 each), and a Kickstarter for the collected and facsimile edition at the end are the strongest fit. With no existing poetry audience, plan on hundreds to low thousands of dollars in year one, not a living.
- **Literary credibility needs a separate pool of poems that never go online, as text or as images.** *Poetry* (pays $500 per poem), *Poetry Northwest* and Palette Poetry count any online appearance, including social media, as publication. *Rattle* is the main exception: it accepts poems that were only self-posted. Chapbook contests (Rattle, Omnidawn, BPJ, Gold Line, Frontier, Cow Creek and others) usually accept poems that were published individually, but the manuscript must be unpublished as a whole.
- **For rights, register GRUW batches before posting.** Under the Copyright Office's guidance, a poem is "published" online only when you authorize people to keep copies, for example by download. Viewing or display alone doesn't count. Selling prints, books or PDFs does publish a poem. In the US the letterforms of the handwriting font can't be copyrighted, but the font software can. NFTs are a collapsed market with reputational baggage. Grants such as the NEA Creative Writing Fellowship (up to $50,000 under the NEA's FY2026 guidelines, with poetry next offered in FY2027) require a prior publication record, so the reserve-for-journals strategy also feeds grant eligibility.

## Key Findings

### 1. Two different meanings of "published"
- **Copyright Office:** Compendium (Third) §1008.3(B) says a work is "published" when it is made available online *and the copyright owner authorizes the end user to retain copies*. A public display or performance alone, such as viewing or streaming, is not publication. Offering copies to intermediaries for further distribution is publication. The Office generally accepts the applicant's own statement that website content is published or unpublished unless that statement is implausible.\[1\] Display-only Instagram posts and a view-only paywalled web piece are therefore arguably unpublished for copyright purposes. Downloadable PDFs, sold prints, books and zines are publication.
- **Journals:** Many journals use a much broader test. *Poetry* (Poetry Foundation) says: "Work that has appeared online for any reason, including social media posts, is considered previously published."\[2\] *Poetry Northwest* and Palette Poetry say the same about blogs and social media.\[3\]\[4\]\[5\] Rattle's older guidelines put it this way: "If you no longer have the power to remove a poem from public view, then that means it's been published."\[6\] Rattle now accepts self-posted, "uncurated" work.\[7\]\[8\]
- **Paywalls and member-only posts:** Treat these as published for strict journals. In a survey of markets about Patreon, *Strange Horizons* said content locked to patrons didn't count as publication, while *Beyond Ceaseless Skies* said it did.\[9\] Black Fox Literary Magazine says anything posted online, including behind a Patreon paywall, counts, and that an excerpt posted online counts as a published excerpt.\[10\] This means posting a fragment or a draft layer can also burn that text.
- **Implication:** The GRUW-before-posting plan is sound, and the copyright side is the easy part. The real limit is that every poem, draft, fragment or scan that appears anywhere online, paywalled or not, should be assumed lost to strict journals.

### 2. The handwriting font
- Under US law, a typeface itself can't be copyrighted ("typeface as typeface," 37 CFR §202.1(e)). The Office also lists "lettering, calligraphy, and typographic ornamentation" as not registrable. Since 1992 the Copyright Office has registered scalable font *software* (OTF, TTF and similar) as a computer program.\[11\]\[12\]\[13\] The "Etceter4 Diary" OTF and WOFF2 files can be protected and licensed. The letter shapes cannot. Your protection comes from the EULA and from copyright in the software. Other countries treat typeface designs differently (Monotype's survey of national laws).\[14\]\[15\]
- **Marketplaces:**
  - MyFonts pays 50% on MyFonts sales and 25% on other Monotype channels. It requires an application. It won't sell fonts offered free elsewhere, and your MyFonts price must match or beat your other channels.\[16\]
  - Creative Market pays shops 50% of list price by default.\[17\]
  - Gumroad's pricing page lists "10% + $0.50 per transaction for all sales through your profile or direct links." Card processing of about 2.9% + $0.30 is charged on top, and sales through Gumroad's Discover marketplace cost 30%.
- **Outcomes vary widely.** Monotype told *The Hustle* that about 45% of its MyFonts foundries say they make a living from font sales and 55% say it's passive income. A designer with a bestseller (Set Sail Studios) averaged about $7,000 a month.\[18\] That figure comes from a polished display font with broad commercial use. A personal handwriting font tied to an art project is a niche product.

### 3. NFT and blockchain editions: the market collapsed, so mostly avoid them
- DappRadar's data shows art-NFT trading volume falling from about $2.9 billion in 2021 to $197 million in 2024, then $23.8 million in Q1 2025. The 2021 top collections lost about 95% of their volume. SuperRare's volume fell 94%.\[19\]\[20\]
- Foundation (foundation.app) shut down in April 2026 after a planned sale to Blackdove fell through. According to ARTnews, it had facilitated $230 million in sales since launch.\[21\]\[22\] Zora moved from NFTs to tradable "content coins" in 2025, which angered artists who had been minting there.\[23\]
- Poetry NFTs did have real high points. Sotheby's sold Ana María Caballero's poem "Cord" for 0.28 BTC (about $11,430) in January 2024, the first individual poem the auction house had sold. Sasha Stiles's piece sold for 3 ETH in the Christie's x Gucci sale in November 2023. The Poetry Society of America showed theVERSEverse works in its storefront window in 2023.\[24\]\[25\]\[26\]
- These successes came from founders who were well known within crypto culture, and they often involved generative or AI collaboration. That aesthetic doesn't fit your rule that all creative text must be your own. Some literary readers also see crypto as a reputational negative, and the platforms themselves are disappearing.
- **Verdict:** At most, treat this as an optional late experiment, for example a Tezos/objkt edition of one poem's version stack. Don't build the model around it.

### 4. Memberships, newsletters and crowdfunding
- **Patreon:** In its June 16, 2025 announcement, Patreon said it was "consolidating our Pro (8%) and Premium (12%) plans into one standard (10%) plan." Creators who launch after August 4, 2025 pay "a new, standard platform rate of 10% on all earnings + applicable fees and taxes." Processing adds about 2.9% + $0.30 per pledge, or 5% + $0.10 on pledges of $3 or less. That usually comes to about 12–17% of gross in total.\[27\]
- **Substack:** Substack takes 10% plus Stripe fees of about 2.9% plus $0.30.\[28\]
- **Realistic Substack numbers for poets:**
  - Poet and teacher Alex Dawson reached $10,000 in annual Substack revenue. Paid subscribers mostly bought monthly 1.5-hour Zoom writing circles with feedback, not just poems.\[29\]
  - A paid poetry Substack called *Poetica* reported 288 free subscribers, 4 paid subscribers and $11 in total payouts after six months.\[30\]\[31\]
  - Substack's own guidance is that "we tend to see 5-10% of free subscribers convert to paying subscriptions." An analysis of public publication data by Really Good Business Ideas, however, found that "the median paid subscriber conversion rate is 3%," and only 20% of publications converted more than 5%. The lesson is that poems alone rarely convert readers to paying, while teaching, access and objects do.
- **Kickstarter:** Kickstarter charges a 5% platform fee plus Stripe processing. In the US, processing is 3% + $0.20 on pledges of $10 or more and 5% + $0.05 on pledges under $10, so the total is roughly 8–10% (per the PledgeBox and The Scale Up Lab 2026 fee guides). Recent poetry campaigns:
  - Joy Moore's poetry collection raised $5,141 from 44 backers (Nov–Dec 2025).\[32\]
  - JJ Brinski's *Terrestrial Poems* raised $9,583 on a $7,800 goal (May–June 2026).\[33\]
  - An illustrated children's poetry anthology from Bandersnatch Books raised $31,330 from 397 backers (2025).\[34\]
  - Small poetry campaigns typically raise $5,000–$10,000, mostly from the author's existing network.

### 6. Grants, prizes and residencies
- **NEA Creative Writing Fellowships:** The program alternates between prose and poetry each year. FY2025 poetry fellowships were $25,000. The NEA's FY2026 guidelines say "fellowships in poetry will be offered in FY 2027," with awards of up to $50,000 and about 35 expected, so the next poetry cycle will probably pay up to $50,000 rather than $25,000. The NEA says it typically receives "approximately 2,000 applications each year" and awards fellowships "to fewer than 2% of applicants." Applicants must already be *published* creative writers. The journal credits you earn from the reserve pool are what make you eligible.
- **Electronic Literature Organization:** The Robert Coover Award for a Work of Electronic Literature pays $1,000 for first place.\[48\] The 2025 winner was *Espejo de Carne*.\[49\] Everest Pipkin's *Anonymous Animal*, a free-to-read web work first published in *the html review*, won the New Media Writing Prize (2022) and the Coover Award (2023).\[50\] This shows that freely accessible web works get e-lit recognition. Paywalling the web piece would mostly limit that recognition.
- **Chapbook contests with real payouts:**
  - Rattle Chapbook Prize: three winners each get $5,000 and 500 copies, distributed to Rattle's 8,000+ subscribers.\[51\]\[52\]
  - Cow Creek: $1,000 and 25 copies, $15 fee.\[53\]
  - BPJ Chad Walsh: print run of about 1,500.\[54\]
  - Others: Omnidawn, Gold Line Press (open Aug 15–Sep 30, 2026), Frontier Poetry Debut ($25 fee), Two Sylvias, Oversound ($18 fee).\[55\]\[56\]\[57\]\[58\]\[59\]
- **Other funders:** Your state arts council, the Academy of American Poets and Rhizome are worth checking each cycle. I didn't verify their current program terms for this report.

## Case Studies

- **Rupi Kaur:** Kaur posted on Instagram and Tumblr and self-published *milk and honey* in November 2014. Accounts of her self-published sales differ: *Publishers Weekly* says more than 10,000 copies, and Kaur herself said 18,000. Andrews McMeel reissued it in October 2015. It passed 1 million print copies by January 2017 and has since sold more than 11 million.\[60\]\[61\]\[62\]\[63\] **What it teaches:** a self-published book backed by an existing social audience and spoken-word touring can lead to a press deal. Her press was a gift-book publisher, not a literary press, which shows that the commercial and literary-credibility tracks really are separate.\[64\]
- **Nayyirah Waheed:** Waheed self-published *salt.* after struggling to find a publisher. The poems were already popular on social media.\[65\] Her model was social posting followed by self-published books, with little extra merchandising.
- **Tyler Knott Gregson:** Gregson is the closest precedent for this project. His "Typewriter Series" is a *numbered* series of poems typed on found paper, now in the thousands (for example "Typewriter Series #3072").\[66\] Tumblr brought him more than 100,000 followers.\[67\] Perigee/Penguin published *Chasers of the Light* in 2014, and it has more than 120,000 copies in print.\[68\] He also sold custom commissioned poems\[66\] and now runs a Substack.\[68\] **What it teaches:** a numbered series in a visible handmade medium builds collectors and a recognizable format. It led to trade-press deals and commissions, not literary-journal prestige.
- **theVERSEverse (Caballero, Stiles, Iwamoto):** Their NFT poetry gallery got Tezos grants, a Sotheby's sale and a Poetry Society of America storefront show.\[26\]\[69\]\[70\] It did all this as the NFT platforms around it collapsed. It's credible within crypto culture but is a weak model for a new account outside that world.
- **Everest Pipkin, *Anonymous Animal*:** A free web work that won two major e-lit prizes.\[50\] This is the model for the interactive piece: keep it open and use it for prizes and reputation.
- **Atticus and Robert M. Drake:** These Instapoets built large followings and trade-book careers. I couldn't verify their monetization figures for this report.

## Where the Goals Conflict

1. **Audience vs. journal eligibility:** Any public posting, paywalled posting or posted fragment rules a poem out at *Poetry*, *Poetry Northwest*, Palette and similar journals.\[2\]\[3\]\[4\]\[5\] **Fix:** split the corpus. *Rattle* is the one top market that welcomes self-posted, uncurated poems, so send your strongest *posted* poems there.\[7\]\[8\]\[71\]
2. **Versions layer vs. reserve:** Showing drafts publicly also publishes those drafts.\[10\] The safest course is to post no version of any reserve poem, including scans, until a journal has accepted it and published it first.
3. **Self-published collected edition vs. chapbook and book contests:** Contests require the *collection* to be unpublished. Omnidawn requires a manuscript to be at least 50% new if part of it appeared in a self-published book.\[56\] Once you self-publish the 100, that arrangement is out of contests. A *different* manuscript built from reserve poems plus a selection of posted poems stays eligible, because individual poems may be previously published.\[51\]\[52\]\[53\]\[57\]
4. **Paid web piece vs. e-lit reputation:** Gating the piece earns little money and keeps it from the open readership that e-lit juries and anthologies reward.
5. **Font sales vs. brand exclusivity:** If anyone can buy the font, "handwritten" posts in that font stop being distinctive. MyFonts also requires matching prices across channels and won't list fonts offered free elsewhere.\[16\]
6. **Participation vs. ownership:** Contributors' text is their copyright. Without a written license you can't include it in paid products.

## Monetizing "n/100" and "Versions"

- **Numbered poem = numbered object:** Each poem n can be offered in a small edition, for example 10 giclée prints of the scan or new handwritten fair copy, signed "n/100 · k/10." Offer one unique original fair copy per poem. Don't sell the 2014–15 journal itself. It is your primary source and the provenance for everything else.
- **Buying a version:** Sell a print of a specific version layer, such as "v1 2014 hand" or "v3 2026 revision." This fits the version-stack concept and costs nothing extra to make.
- **Subscriber access to layers:** Free followers see the final poem. Members see the full version stack, footnotes, the critical/context layer and the then-vs-now reflections. Collector members also get one print a quarter.
- **Collector tier:** A limited "full-run" subscription, for example 25 slots, gives each collector a matched print of every one of the 100. That builds a complete numbered set.
- **Collected edition:** A trade edition, a signed and numbered limited edition, and a deluxe facsimile of the notebook with an annotation booklet, funded through Kickstarter.

## Participation Without Diluting Ownership

- **Keep the 100 single-authored.** Run participation as a parallel layer, such as "responses" or "marginalia," that doesn't count toward the 100. This keeps your no-AI, own-text rule clean and avoids co-authorship questions. GRUW only covers works by the same author or co-authors.\[72\]
- **Formats:**
  - Prompts you post, with handwritten replies readers photograph and send in.
  - Annotation projects. Caballero had 50 people annotate one poem and kept the physical originals.\[70\]
  - Live writing circles for members.
- **Rights:** Use a short contributor agreement. The contributor keeps copyright and grants you a non-exclusive, royalty-free license to display the response and include it in an anthology, with credit. Get explicit consent for any paid product. Require contributors to confirm the text is their own and not AI-generated, matching *Poetry*'s and *Rattle*'s human-written policies.\[2\]\[7\]\[8\] Note for contributors that your display may count as "published" for their own journal submissions.

## Recommendations: Phased Plan

**Phase 0 – Before the identity launches (next 2–4 months)**
1. Sort the corpus into three pools:
   - **Series**: about 100 poems to be posted.
   - **Reserve**: 15–30 of the strongest poems, never posted in any form.
   - **Archive**: the journal pages, kept back for the facsimile.
2. Submit Reserve poems now, in batches of 3–6, to strict and paying journals. *Poetry* is closed from June 15 to September 15, and its How to Submit page says: "We receive more than 100,000 poems each year… response time is up to one year." As of the March 2026 issue it pays $500 per text or visual poem and $600 per video poem. Send Series poems you want credit for to *Rattle*, which accepts self-posted work.\[8\]
3. File GRUW registrations for Series batches (10 poems, $85, all unpublished) before posting each batch.\[73\]\[74\] Keep a log of which version was registered. Later revisions add new authorship that the earlier registration doesn't cover.
4. Lock down the name, handles and domain, and run a basic trademark search. Set up your *own* site and an email list from the first day, because platform followers aren't an audience you own.
5. Keep the font exclusive to the project. Draft a EULA now.

**Phase 1 – During the series (about 12–24 months, 1–2 poems a week)**
1. Post each n/100 free with minimal UI, and link each post to the canonical page on your site.
2. Start a membership at $5 a month for the version and notes layers. Add a $15–$20 tier with a monthly live writing circle, which is your best near-term income. Add per-poem print editions once a poem has 10 or more interested buyers.
3. Launch participation as a separate layer under a written contributor license.
4. Keep the interactive web piece free. Submit it to the ELO awards and e-lit venues such as *the html review*.
5. When eligible, apply for state arts council and other grants. Prepare for NEA poetry fellowships (the NEA's FY2026 guidelines say poetry will be offered in FY2027, with awards of up to $50,000 and fewer than 2% of roughly 2,000 applicants funded) once your journal credits meet the threshold.

**Phase 2 – After poem 100**
1. Run a Kickstarter for the collected and facsimile editions. Set a realistic goal of $5,000–$10,000 unless the list is large.
2. Build a *separate* chapbook manuscript from Reserve poems plus selected Series poems in a new arrangement for contests (Rattle, Omnidawn, BPJ, Cow Creek, Frontier and others).
3. License the font on your own store (via Gumroad, which charges 10% + $0.50 per transaction plus about 2.9% + $0.30 card processing), or apply to MyFonts (50%) if you want the exposure and accept its pricing rules.
4. Start the roots-and-glyphs linguistics phase as a new numbered series, and reuse the same model.

**Realistic revenue (my estimates, not sourced data):**
- Year 1 with no existing poetry audience: about $0–$3,000 in total. Most of it would come from a few dozen members, workshops and a handful of prints.
- Year 2: about $2,000–$10,000 if posting builds a list of a few thousand people, plus a Kickstarter at the end.
- Grants and prizes are lumpy, and most of them depend on having journal credits first.

**Avoid:**
- Paywalling the core work.
- Selling or cutting up the original notebook.
- Posting drafts of Reserve poems.
- Self-publishing the full collection before you've tried contests with a different manuscript.
- NFTs as a core model.
- Opening per-poem paid contributions that blur authorship.
- Pricing commissions below about $40, because handwritten work is labor-limited.

## Caveats
- This is informational, not legal advice. The Copyright Office's online-publication analysis depends on the facts, so have an attorney review the font EULA and contributor terms.
- Journal policies change. Check each journal's guidelines when you submit. Some editors treat paywalled or member-only posts differently.\[9\]
- Some figures come from secondary or SEO-type sources: Substack conversion rates, full-year NFT totals, and Etsy prices, which are live listings with no date. Kaur's self-published sales figures conflict between sources (10,000 vs. 18,000). Monetization data for Atticus and Robert M. Drake, Ko-fi and Buy Me a Coffee fees, reading honoraria, and Academy of American Poets and Rhizome program terms weren't verified for this report.
